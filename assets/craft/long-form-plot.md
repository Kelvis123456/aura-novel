# Long-form plot

## When to use
Use when outlining a book, volume, or arc; choosing a structure; designing conflict, pacing, and escalation; planning payoffs, reversals, and twists; or shaping the emotional line of a long, serialized story.

## Core rules
- Decide the target emotion first, then pick a proven pattern that delivers it. Every scene must serve a stated emotion; a scene that cannot name its emotion should not exist.
- Outline in three layers: the outline (a few hundred words, never abandoned), the plot outline (full storyline, adjustable), and the detail layer (dialogue, small scenes, fully free). Keep detail outlines to roughly a third of the final prose length, and mark each node as expand or skim.
- Build every volume by reverse engineering: fix a one-line ending and the climax first (largest conflict, most characters, strongest emotion), then work back through goals and setups. Plan expectation first, payoff second, setup third.
- Use eight beats per volume: opening 5-10%, development 30-40%, first turn 5-10%, second turn 5-10% with higher stakes of a different kind, climax 10-15%, aftermath of the conflict 10-15%, last turn 5-10%, ending 10-15%. Do not resolve every thread at the end.
- Hold the story's core as one goal, not an element. The main line is one thing the protagonist wants; leveling is only the action toward it. Every side line must feed the main line.
- Run an upward spiral of small cycles: stable state with latent danger, crisis trigger, breakthrough action, reward, new balance with the next problem planted. Each win carries a new cost or risk, and the core conflict is never solved before the finale.
- Escalate conflict in kind: words, then action, then fierce confrontation, then decisive result. Difficulty must rise; flat is monotony and falling is boredom. Near the goal, add the unexpected: a plan failing, an improvisation, or a hidden card.
- Give the main conflict a binding agent so characters cannot simply walk away: a reason to kill, a professional duty, a moral duty, or a confined place. Choose one main stake (physical, career, or psychological death) and keep it for the whole book.
- Press on the system, not the person, when designing an antagonist: the villain has a goal of their own that harms the protagonist as a side effect. Show the villain's plan to readers when it builds anticipation; do not speak in riddles.
- Escalate by thresholds: split the goal into resource, achievement, collection, or consumption gates, announce them in batches, and set the next one as soon as one is crossed. Tie gates to the core selling point.
- Interleave a growth line with a conflict line: grow stronger, pull out a conflict, grow stronger, get drawn in, grow stronger, clash, resolve, collect. Keep the protagonist slightly ahead in each small exchange and win big at the end.
- Rotate patterns and never repeat the same payoff three times without variation; same-type plots need at least three different plots between them, and consecutive units must use different uses of the protagonist's edge.
- Run two or three conflict lines at once, with the long arcs planted early, and always keep one short expectation and one or two long ones open.
- Stage the climax in five steps: build energy, false win, collapse, crosscutting struggle, suspended finish. A big climax runs roughly 14 chapters; a small one about 3 days of reading, followed by one or two quiet chapters.
- Assign each chapter a position: high-pressure chapters about 15-20%, regular advance 40-50%, training and trial 5-10%, relationship payoff 5-10%, low-pressure life up to 10%, information chapters up to 5%. Fast, male-oriented serials can run above 30% high-pressure. Low-pressure chapters may skip payoffs but must still give a reason to read on.
- Put one key event at the quarter, midpoint, and three-quarter marks of every volume. Do not run the same emotional motif for more than two or three chapters in a row.
- Choose an emotional arc by effect: V shape (trough at 60-70%) for suffering then triumph, inverted V (peak at 40-50%, faster fall) for tragedy, W shape (maximum three swells, each higher) for many twists, staircase for escalating comeuppance, delayed release (70-80% pressure) for hidden strength, sharp turn at 70-80%. Combine at most two.
- Reversals must be fair: at least three earlier clues, driven by a character's choice, never by coincidence, and revealed in under about 300 words. Best timing is 70-85% of the piece; never before 50% or after 95%. Cut to one or two twists done well.
- Pick the reversal type: identity, perspective (the narrator sincerely believes a partial truth), motive (a forced choice exposes it), timeline (reorder, never lie), information (one false fact is overturned), perception (the whole feeling toward a person flips), or none, for sweet and retribution stories.
- Mislead by adding believable false layers or by hiding part of the information, never by lying. A red herring must still do real work in the plot.
- Design emotion as three layers: what the character feels, what the text conveys, and what the reader actually feels. Alternate pulling emotion up and releasing it; pressure without release breeds frustration, release without pressure feels flat.
- Build bonds with concrete numbers, objects, and repeated habits in the first third, tear them in the middle, and end with a quiet detail rather than speeches. Change emotional direction every three to five beats, each with a trigger.
- Keep the five things that stop a long book collapsing: the goal never disappears, the selling point never shrinks, the protagonist always has social ties, there is always someone above to reach for, and growth never stops.

## Diagnose
- Can I name the exact emotion this arc or scene delivers?
- What is the protagonist's one-sentence goal, and does every line serve it?
- Is there a next unresolved problem planted before this one closes?
- Does difficulty rise from obstacle to obstacle?
- Where are the quarter, midpoint, and three-quarter events?
- Have I given the reader pressure and release in balance, or one without the other?
- Is the same payoff pattern repeating without variation?
- Could the twist be guessed in retrospect from three earlier clues?
- Are two or more expectation lines alive right now?
- Does any chapter exist only to move information?

## How to apply
Reply in the language of the request. For "plan this arc": state the target emotion, a one-sentence ending, the climax, then the eight beats with approximate chapter counts, the quarter, midpoint, and three-quarter events, chapter positions, and the planted threads. For a chapter-level plan add purpose, expand or skim, and the closing hook. For "fix this plot": run the diagnosis, name the failure, and rewrite the outline segment. For a twist or emotional arc: choose the type, give the setup beats with positions, the reveal, and the aftermath. Keep it concrete and use the user's own characters.

## Deep notes
Five-level outlining. Level one is the whole book in a sentence. Level two is one story line per main arc. Level three is concrete plot. Stop at three for long fiction; deeper levels drift from the main line. Always expand from the top down, never from details upward.

Core loop design. Combine genre, edge, and protagonist identity into one loop, such as case chain (case, clue, partial truth, bigger mystery), hide-and-strike (develop quietly, provocation, crush, shock), resource spiral (resource, skill, strength, new place, new resource), or road structure (travel, meet, travel). Keep the loop's fuel alive: if the engine is information gaps, never let the gaps close.

Buffer zones. Distance between the protagonist and others, created by interest, event, or feeling, gives satisfaction when closed. Show side characters repeatedly discovering the protagonist. Reveal in this order: bystanders, a minor rival, a stronger rival, the main opponent, the protagonist's crushing finish, then the larger boss.

Three-act, four-act, five-act. Use three acts when the world's conflict is strong without the protagonist pushing. Use four, rise-turn-resolve, when outside force pushes a passive protagonist. Use five when a cornered protagonist strikes back. The five-act chain must have five links with no skipping: seed, growth, turn, sprint, resolution. A turn is a qualitative worsening of the conflict, and its cause is planted early.

Aftermath act. Showing off is conflict between the protagonist and society's perception. When real power exceeds social perception, a display closes the gap. Prefer having environment, others, and time say the protagonist is strong rather than the protagonist claiming it.

Reversal setup examples. Identity: seed one hint at 5%, another at 25%, a third at 50%, reveal at 75% by scene, never by confession. Motive: the stated reason points to option A, a high-pressure choice picks B. Perception: let readers accumulate hatred, bury one small contrary detail in each cruel scene, then reveal a single object that reverses them all. Nested reversals: first at 55-65%, second at 80-90%, with a beat of digestion between, and the final one shortest and sharpest.

Arc word budgets for 12,000 words. V shape: start 10%, descent 30%, trough 15%, turn 10%, climb 25%, finish 10%. Inverted V: start 10%, climb 35%, peak 10%, reversal 5%, fall 30%, close 10%. Delayed release: suppression 20%, deep setup 25%, maximum pressure 15%, trigger 5%, burst 25%, close 10%.

Mid-story pressure tools: public escalation, double betrayal, accelerating cost of inaction, strategic silence.

Weak versus strong. Weak: the protagonist loses eight fights in a row across thirty thousand words. Strong: four losses, summarized, with the protagonist confidently predicting the opposite. 


Genre structure choices. A strong main line, with a full arc in every volume and nested smaller arcs, suits upgrade and mystery stories where people serve the plot. A weak main line, a chain of self-contained episodes with the protagonist as the thread, suits invincible-hero and slice-of-life stories where character novelty carries the book. Many books open as episodes and tighten into a strong main line. Whichever you choose, no side line may exist without a link to the main line.

Changing settings without losing readers. Lay relationships before moving: a new place should already have a character or legend touching the protagonist. Carry at least one unfinished goal across, bring important people along, and let each new setting raise the loop: bigger scale, higher gates, stronger opponents. Build a top-down world first, with the leading powers as pillars, and keep a yardstick so the first boss counts as 5, the middle as 50, and only the late story reaches 100.

Love lines inside plot. Choose either a dramatic process (forbidden love, regret chase) or a dramatic pairing of two personalities, and keep the interaction pattern consistent to the end. Run four phases: meeting, comedy of attraction (probing, 30-50% of the line, each probe readable as sweet and sour), separation by outside pressure or a revealed truth, and a final stretch with no new obstacles.

Spiral of payoff. When a pattern returns, raise its complexity and its reward. Escalate in range (individual, group, society), depth (surface, essence, overturn), and rank of those shocked. Prefer a long expectation over a quick release: curiosity, worry, and resentment last longer than the thrill itself.
