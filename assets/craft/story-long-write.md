# story-long-write

## When to use
Use for long serialized novels written chapter by chapter: building a book from premise to outline to chapters, continuing an existing manuscript, planning volumes, managing pacing, hooks, cliffhangers, chapter endings, payoff beats and consistency across dozens or hundreds of chapters.

## Core rules
- Decide the target emotion first, then the story. Every scene must deliver a nameable emotion (release, shock, longing, tension, anticipation). A scene whose emotion you cannot name should not exist.
- Start from proven patterns, not blank invention. Find the genre's verified plot module, treat reference characters as functions (rival, ally, catalyst), and fill those slots with your own people and material. Never copy specific scenes.
- Load only what the chapter needs: involved characters' states, foreshadowing due, relevant world rules.
- Plan in layers: book (volumes), volume (rhythm, emotional arc, foreshadowing, reversals), chapter (beats, hooks). Build chapter outlines in batches of about 10, then roll forward every 5 chapters. Never skip a chapter's outline.
- Each volume fixes its emotion, core conflict, where pacing speeds and slows, seeds planted and paid off, and moves the protagonist from state A to a clearly different state B.
- Give every chapter a role: high pressure, advancing, trial and error, relationship payoff, low-pressure life, or information sorting. Only high-pressure and advancing chapters need a full hook, payoff and suspense stack. Quiet chapters may have a weak or emotional hook, but every chapter must give the reader a reason to continue. Keep low-pressure and filler chapters under about 15% of the total, and never let adjacent chapters carry the same emotion.
- Anchor the quarter, midpoint and three-quarter points of a volume with one key event each.
- Chapter length: default minimum 3,000 words; fast-advance and climax chapters at least 2,000. Land between the target and 1.1 times it; if under 90%, expand thin dense beats, never pad.
- Budget words per beat. Dense beats (payoff, face-slap, reversal, emotional peak) get at least 250 words, slow-motion payoffs 400 to 600. Sparse beats (transitions, travel, exposition) about 40. Setup and daily life about 120 to 150. Alternate density; never spend equal effort on every beat.
- Payoff rhythm: in high-pressure stretches, one satisfying emotional beat every 3,000 to 5,000 words. The longer the reader waited, the cleaner and harder the release.
- Build payoff by working backward: define the release, then plant a visible danger or expectation before it. No setup means an empty payoff. Setup matters more than the blow itself.
- Showing-off or face-slap cycle (about 5 chapters): establish persona 1, setup 2, payoff 1, aftermath 1. Prepare the hero's tools and strength before the confrontation so the reader is not anxious. Target someone weighty, and show varied bystander reactions, not only the protagonist's action.
- Upgrade feeling: define start and end status, then plant the gap in reverse (scarcity, envy, visible effect). Contrast before and after on several dimensions; keep major power-ups few.
- Open every chapter with a hook in the first 100 words, never weather or scenery unless the contrast is sharp. Chapter-start hooks: suggestive dialogue, flash-forward fragment, countdown, mysterious monologue, contrasting scenes, interrupted action, imagery foreshadowing.
- End every chapter on something that makes the reader turn the page. Chapter-end hooks: sudden reveal, urgent crisis, unfinished action, identity reversal, dilemma, mystery object, countdown, promise or threat, impossible disappearance, hidden meaning, recurring image changed, echo of the opening with one detail altered, withheld reaction. About 100 words is enough.
- Hook strength: strong in chapters 1 to 3 and before climaxes, medium in mid-story daily chapters. Never repeat a hook type in consecutive chapters.
- Early chapters: externalize inner change into visible events (decision, misjudgment, dialogue, object, pressure), never long monologue.
- Keep close, limited perspective in the present moment; do not step outside to explain, preview or uplift. Prefer strong emotion and sharp dialogue over mild versions.
- Dialogue must advance plot or reveal character; in grief or danger, drop comic voices and have each line answer the previous emotion.
- Fights are strategy and reversal, not blow-by-blow. Daily scenes need interaction and seeds.
- Keep a character state file (identity, ability, key relationships, public image, pending seeds, change log), a foreshadowing ledger, and a timeline. Update all three after every chapter. Track only recurring characters.
- Reversal: give hope, then break it (false win, then collapse), with seeds planted earlier.
- Never mention writing machinery inside the story text: no "chapter", "previous chapter", "foreshadowing", "outline", "reader". Refer to events and objects the characters can perceive.
- Avoid negate-then-affirm constructions ("not X, but Y"), generic similes, repeated body-part cliches, and summary morals at chapter ends.

## Planning checklist
- What single emotion does this chapter deliver, and what pattern delivers it reliably?
- What role does this chapter play (pressure, advance, trial, relationship, low pressure, sorting)?
- What is the word target, and how is it split across dense and sparse beats?
- What do the present characters know, want and have (state file)?
- Which planted seeds are due to advance or pay off here?
- What are the opening and ending hooks, and do they differ from the last chapter's?
- Cause, action, result, new problem: is the chain complete?
- Who reacts to the key event, and how does each differ?
- Does the payoff have a visible danger or expectation planted before it?
- What changes in relationships or status by chapter end?
- Does this chapter's emotion differ from the one before it?

## How to apply
Write the full chapter as finished prose, never an outline or summary, at or above the target length. Obey the beat budgets: expand payoff and reversal beats slowly, move through transitions fast. Begin in the middle of the action with the hook already working; do not recap the previous chapter. Continue from the last paragraph of prior text exactly where it stopped, reusing established names, facts, tone and sentence rhythm, and never contradicting the state, foreshadowing and timeline notes provided. Do not repeat earlier scenes or restate earlier events; only add new events and consequences. End on a hook consistent with the chapter's role, leaving the next move open without explaining it. Use short paragraphs, one line break between paragraphs, dialogue on its own lines. Do not use ellipses or dashes for pauses; use short sentences or a gesture. When asked for several chapters, write them in order, each complete, each with a different hook type. Output only story text, with no commentary or self-checks beyond the chapter title.
