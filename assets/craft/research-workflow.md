# research-workflow
## When to use
Use when a story needs accurate, vivid real-world material: historical periods, professions, places, technology, medicine, law, customs, slang, procedures. It turns a vague "I need to know about X" into usable, honest research notes the author can draw on while writing.

## Core rules
- Convert the topic into specific, answerable sub-questions before gathering anything. "Victorian hospitals" becomes: what did a ward look like, who ran it, what were the routines, what treatments existed in this exact year, what did it smell and sound like.
- Define scope first: what is in, what is out, and what the scene or chapter actually needs.
- Work in passes, not in one shot: broad overview first, specific details second, verification and counter-views last.
- Use a pattern that fits the question: funnel (overview, specifics, details), multi-perspective (proponents, critics, neutral), temporal (past, present, trend), geographic (global, regional, local).
- Do at least three to five distinct passes or queries for anything beyond a simple fact; a quick lookup needs one to two, a deep dive twenty or more.
- Record after each pass: what was asked, two or three key findings, notable sources, and the new questions raised.
- Prefer primary sources (first-hand accounts, official documents, original data, period texts) over secondary ones (articles, blogs, summaries, textbooks).
- Weigh every source with the CRAAP test: Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose.
- Watch for bias signals: funding, affiliation, commercial interest, missing counterarguments, sensational tone, no author, no date.
- Cross-check surprising or load-bearing claims against a second independent source before treating them as fact.
- Sort findings by theme, then note consensus, conflicts, gaps and trends.
- Label confidence on each finding: High (several authoritative sources agree), Medium (some evidence, few sources), Low (single source, anecdote, conflict or dated).
- State limitations plainly: what could not be found, what is assumed, where sources are biased.
- Never invent sources, quotes, statistics, dates, names or page numbers. If a source cannot be named, say it is from general knowledge.
- Without web access, work from memory and reasoning only: say so at the top, give what is generally known, mark every specific figure as unverified, and list what the author should check.
- Guard against the classic pitfalls: premature synthesis, using only similar sources, recency bias, oversimplifying nuance, and seeking only confirming evidence.
- End with story-usable output: concrete sensory details, terminology, plausible procedures, common errors to avoid, and open questions.
- Distinguish fact from fictional license, so the author knows which details are deliberate departures.

## Diagnose
- What exactly does the story need to know, and what is the scene that will use it?
- Which sub-questions are unanswered, and which have been answered only vaguely?
- Is this a factual, exploratory, comparative or decision question?
- What time period, place and social level must the details fit?
- Which claims are load-bearing for plot, and have they been cross-checked?
- Where do sources disagree, and does the disagreement help or hurt the story?
- What is remembered from general knowledge versus actually verified?
- Which details are vivid and specific, and which are generic filler?
- What common misconceptions or anachronisms could readers catch?
- What remains unknown, and how could the author resolve it?

## How to apply
Reply in the language of the request. Return research notes, not an essay. Open with a one-line scope and a note on method (for example "no web access: based on general knowledge, unverified specifics are marked"). Then give: sub-questions and findings grouped by theme, each with a confidence label and the source type or named source; a short list of consensus, disagreements and gaps; story-ready details (sensory, vocabulary, procedures, typical mistakes); and open questions with suggested ways to verify them. Keep it proportional to the request: a quick fact gets a few lines. Never fabricate citations; when the author supplies sources or pasted text, base the notes on them and quote them accurately.

## Deep notes
The four phases. Planning: write the research question, three to five sub-questions, scope in and out, a query order from broad to specific to verification, and success criteria (every sub-question answered, key findings confirmed by more than one source, gaps identified, output ready to synthesize). Execution: run the passes in order and log each; iterate when a finding raises a new question, contradicts the plan or looks too good to be true. Analysis: group by theme, mark consensus versus conflict, rate confidence, list limitations. Synthesis: lead with the most important findings, connect related ideas, show confidence, acknowledge limits, then finish with takeaways, recommendations and further questions.

Research types and focus. Factual: accuracy, recency, authoritative sources. Exploratory: breadth, several viewpoints, emerging patterns. Comparative: consistent criteria and fair representation (for example two eras, two professions, two cities). Decision support: actionability and risk, which for fiction means choosing which version of a detail to commit to.

Source types. Academic: peer review, methodology, institution. News: reputation, multiple sources inside the article, news versus opinion, corrections. Industry or trade: the author's role, conflicts of interest, practical examples, recency in fast-moving fields. Official: confirm it is the official source, check update frequency, look for underlying data. Forums and Q&A: votes, recency, cross-check, author reputation. Rate a source high when authority, accuracy, currency, purpose and objectivity mostly check out; low when it is anonymous, undated, promotional, sensational, self-citing or makes extraordinary claims without evidence. Record each key source as: name, type, credibility, strengths, weaknesses, what it is used for.

Useful frameworks. A five-whys chain gets from a surface fact to the cause that drives behavior (why did the clerk carry that ledger, why was it audited, why did that matter to him). A strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats pass on a topic reveals tension a story can use. Compare and contrast clarifies options.

Notes format for fiction. For each finding: statement, confidence, source or basis, how to use it in a scene. Then a section of "texture": sounds, smells, tools, jargon, daily routines, costs, distances, travel times, what people ate and wore, who held power. Then "traps": anachronisms, popular myths, Hollywood errors. Then "unknowns": what the author must verify or may freely invent.

Weak versus strong. Weak: "Medieval sailors ate hardtack and got scurvy." Strong: separate what is well attested (ship's biscuit was a staple on long voyages; scurvy was a known danger on long voyages) from what varies by century, nation and ship, mark the confidence for each, and give a concrete image the scene can use, flagged as plausible rather than documented.

Time budgets. Quick research is fifteen to thirty minutes of effort, one or two questions and a few key takeaways. Standard is three to five questions, ten to fifteen passes and a structured report. Deep is a comprehensive question set with detailed documentation. Match depth to how much weight the detail carries in the story.

No-web mode. State clearly that nothing could be looked up. Offer reasoning-based answers, general frameworks, typical ranges rather than invented exact numbers, and a verification list with suggested source types (period memoirs, museum collections, professional manuals, official statistics). Treat confident memory as Medium at best unless it is textbook-level common knowledge.
