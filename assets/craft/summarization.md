# summarization
## When to use
Use when an author needs a chapter recap that lets a model continue a long novel without contradicting it, or another kind of summary: a logline, a synopsis, a beat sheet or a brief reminder of earlier events.

## Core rules
- Summarization is translation, not just reduction: pick the summary type that matches its purpose before condensing.
- Clarify purpose, audience, scope, emphasis and format. For continuity recaps the reader is a model that must keep writing, so completeness of facts beats elegance.
- For a continuity recap, capture events in causal order first, then the state changes at the end of the chapter.
- Track who knows what: separate what each character knows, suspects, believes wrongly, and what only the reader knows.
- Track physical state: injuries, illness, fatigue, clothing, hunger, weapons, magic or resource levels, and how long they last.
- Track objects: who holds each important item, where it is, and its condition.
- Track location and time: where each character ends the chapter, the date or time of day, elapsed time, season, travel durations.
- Track relationships: shifts in trust, alliances, debts, attraction, grudges, and the last thing said between key pairs.
- Track promises, threats, deadlines, oaths and secrets, with who made them and to whom.
- List open threads and planted setups separately from resolved ones, so nothing is dropped or resolved twice.
- Record tone and mood at the chapter's end, point-of-view character, tense, and any voice habits the continuation must keep.
- Preserve concrete specifics (names, numbers, exact wording of key lines, unusual details); over-abstraction destroys continuity.
- Never invent information. Mark what is uncertain or ambiguous in the text instead of resolving it.
- Keep reduction proportional: short chapters get short recaps, pivotal chapters get fuller ones.
- Verify the summary against the source for distortion: compression must not change meaning, order or causality.
- Logline: one sentence of roughly 25-35 words giving protagonist, situation, goal and stakes, with no spoilers.
- Synopsis: present tense, third person, covers the whole plot including the ending, with emotional causality (because of this, therefore that).
- Beat sheet: a numbered list of story beats, each one line, showing the turn or change in each, not the scenery.
- Use progressive summary for long books: a one-line gist per chapter, a paragraph per act, and fuller detail only where it will be needed.
- Do not pad with praise, interpretation or advice unless the author asks for a critical summary.

## Diagnose
- What will this summary be used for: continuation, outline, pitch, or reader reminder?
- Who reads it, and what do they already know?
- What would a continuation get wrong if this fact were missing?
- Who knows what at the end of the chapter, and has that changed?
- What physical, material, spatial and temporal state carries into the next scene?
- Which promises, threats and setups remain open?
- Have I kept names, numbers and exact details, or turned them into vague language?
- Did I add anything the text does not say, or smooth over an ambiguity?
- Is the length proportional to the chapter's importance?
- Does the tone line still show how the chapter feels at its end?

## How to apply
Reply in the language of the request. If the author pastes a chapter without further instruction, produce a continuity recap with these labeled sections: Events (numbered, in order), Knowledge (who knows what), Physical state, Objects, Location and time, Relationships, Promises and threats, Open threads, Tone and voice, Continuity flags (contradictions or ambiguities spotted). Be terse and factual, with no prose flourishes. If a different summary is requested, deliver that type: logline (one sentence), synopsis (one to three paragraphs unless a length is given), beat sheet (numbered list), or a one-line gist. Ask one clarifying question only when the purpose is truly unclear; otherwise state the assumption and proceed.

## Deep notes
Why types matter. "Summarize" can mean several things. Key point extraction lists discrete important points. Abstraction groups details into patterns. Gisting keeps only the one thing worth remembering. Compression shortens while keeping everything. Synthesis merges several sources into one account. A critical summary evaluates while it condenses. An actionable summary focuses on what happens next. For fiction, continuity recaps use compression with a fixed checklist; loglines use gisting; synopses use abstraction plus causality; beat sheets use outline structure.

Continuity recap template. Fields: Chapter and point of view; Events (3-12 items, one line each, chronological, with cause and effect visible); Knowledge ledger (character: knows, suspects, wrongly believes, hiding); Body and health (character: injury, where, severity, expected healing); Inventory (item: holder, place, condition); Place and clock (where, date or time of day, days elapsed since the story began); Relationships (pair: current state, last exchange); Promises, threats, deadlines (who, what, to whom, when); Open threads (question or setup planted, not yet paid off); Resolved this chapter; Tone and style (mood at the end, pacing, recurring motifs, speech habits); Flags (anything that contradicts earlier chapters or is left ambiguous on purpose).

Weak versus strong. Weak: "Mara and Tobias argue and she leaves. They are tense." Strong: "Mara tells Tobias she found the ledger in his father's desk; he denies knowing about it, lying (the reader knows he burned the first page). She leaves for the harbor at dusk with the ledger and a cut on her left palm from the broken lamp. He does not know she has read page twelve. Promise: she will meet the harbor master at dawn." The strong version carries knowledge, object, injury, time, promise and dramatic irony.

Long novels. Keep a running layer: a master recap that holds standing facts (ages, appearances, rules of the world, long-term injuries, timeline) and a chapter layer that holds the latest changes. When recaps pile up, merge them and drop details that no longer matter, but never drop open threads or standing facts. Keep the most recent chapters at full detail and older ones compressed.

Loglines. Formula: when a specific protagonist faces an inciting problem, they must pursue a goal or lose something that matters. Include one irony or unusual detail. Avoid names beyond the protagonist, avoid backstory, and avoid rhetorical questions.

Synopsis. Aim for one page unless asked otherwise. Present tense, third person. Open with protagonist and world in a sentence or two, then inciting incident, escalation, midpoint turn, crisis, climax and resolution. Show motive and consequence, not only events. Reveal the ending; a synopsis is not a blurb.

Beat sheet. Number each beat. Name the change in one line: what the protagonist wants now, what blocks it, what turns. Typical beats: opening image, setup, inciting incident, first decision, escalation, midpoint, low point, climax, resolution. Adapt the count to the book's real shape rather than forcing a template.

Edge cases. Unreliable narrators: record both what is said and what the text signals is true. Time jumps and flashbacks: note them with their position on the timeline. Multiple POVs: keep one knowledge ledger per viewpoint character. Ambiguous passages: quote the ambiguous line and do not resolve it. Very short chapters can be merged in one recap entry.

Final check. Compare the recap to the chapter once: is every important fact present, is anything added, is the order right, would a writer continuing from the recap alone make a mistake?
