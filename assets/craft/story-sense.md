# story-sense
## When to use
Use when the writer is stuck, the story feels broken without a clear cause, or they ask "what's wrong with my story?". It finds what state the story is in and picks the single next intervention.

## Core rules
- Story sense is knowing what any story needs regardless of its current state. There is no such thing as "stuck": there is only a problem not yet diagnosed or an intervention not yet applied.
- Work as a loop: Assess, Diagnose, Intervene, Reassess. It is a diagnostic model, not a linear process.
- Listen for the symptoms the writer describes, ask clarifying questions to locate where they are stuck, match symptoms to a state, name the diagnosis, recommend one intervention, and offer a first step.
- State 0, no story: nothing exists yet; generate a seed idea.
- State 1, concept without foundation: the idea exists but world, characters or plot feel thin; fight the generic and build foundations.
- State 2, world without life: the setting is a backdrop; give it beliefs, economy and governance that shape people.
- State 3, flat non-humans: aliens or fantasy species read as humans in costume; develop their differences in language and culture.
- State 4, characters without dimension: characters serve plot instead of driving it; work on inner arc (false belief, want vs. need).
- State 4.5, plot without pacing: scenes work individually but do not accumulate; fix scene sequencing.
- State 5, plot without purpose: events happen but do not accumulate meaning; clarify key moments and moral stakes.
- State 5.5, dialogue feels flat: characters sound alike and conversations are lifeless.
- State 5.75, ending does not land: the build is good but the resolution disappoints.
- State 5.85, draft not progressing: planning is done but drafting is not happening.
- State 5.9, prose feels flat: the story works but sentences are merely functional.
- State 6, draft complete: revision feels overwhelming; triage it.
- State 7, ready for evaluation: the story exists but its quality is uncertain; run analysis and a representation check.
- Decision shortcuts: feels generic, avoid cliche; world thin, build the world; characters flat, character arc; pacing off, scene sequencing; dialogue wooden, dialogue; ending weak, endings; meaning unclear, moral stakes; prose flat, prose style.
- Anti-patterns: prescribing instead of diagnosing (ask first), framework overload (recommend one intervention, expand after reassessing), ignoring the writer's energy (favor what energizes them), treating structure as story (keep asking "does this feel right?").

## Diagnose
- Is there anything on the page yet, or only an idea?
- Does the problem feel generic, thin, flat, slow, hollow or unfinished, and which word fits best?
- Do the scenes work alone but fail to build?
- Do the characters drive events or merely serve them?
- Does the setting matter to the plot, or could it be swapped out?
- Do the characters sound distinct without dialogue tags?
- Does the ending pay off what the opening promised?
- Is the writer blocked on planning, drafting or revising?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First write a diagnosis: the story state you identify, the symptoms in the passage that support it, and your confidence; if the passage is too small to judge, say so and ask at most two clarifying questions. Recommend exactly one intervention and say why it comes first. Then return a rewritten passage that applies that single intervention (for example, adding a driving choice to a passive character, or grounding a backdrop setting in a detail that affects action), keeping the author's plot, names and voice. Close with a one-line suggestion for what to reassess next. Do not rewrite the whole story or stack several frameworks.


## Deep notes

### The six-step diagnostic process
- Listen for symptoms: what exactly does the writer call the problem? Ask clarifying questions to get specific about where they stall (page, scene, chapter, or before drafting). Identify the state. Name the diagnosis aloud and explain what you see. Recommend one intervention. Offer a first step small enough to do today.
- Do not use this approach when the writer wants the story written for them, or wants only coaching questions with no diagnosis.

### Decision tree in order
- Anything on the page? No: generate a seed (see below). Yes: what is the problem?
- Feels generic: break the cliche. World thin: build the world's beliefs, economy and governance. Non-humans fake: develop their language and culture. Characters flat: work the inner arc. Pacing off: resequence scenes. Dialogue wooden: differentiate voices. Ending weak: rework the resolution. Meaning unclear: sharpen moral stakes. Draft stalled: restart the drafting habit. Prose flat: work the sentence level. Draft needs revision: triage.
- Several symptoms at once: pick the earliest state on the ladder. A State 4 problem usually causes the State 5.5 symptom, so fix the lower number first.

### Symptom phrases and what they usually mean
- "It's boring" is often State 4.5 (scenes do not accumulate) or State 4 (a passive protagonist), not a prose problem.
- "I don't know what happens next" with characters present is State 4: ask what the character wants and what they fear, then what choice that forces.
- "Everything happens but nothing matters" is State 5: events lack accumulating meaning, so look for the moral question each key moment poses.
- "It reads fine but I don't care" is also State 4 or 5; "I care until the last chapter" is State 5.75.
- "I have it all outlined and cannot write it" is State 5.85: the block is drafting, not planning. Do not send them back to outlining.
- "It's good but not memorable" is State 5.9.
- "I have 80,000 words and cannot face them" is State 6: the problem is overwhelm, so triage rather than prescribe.

### Randomness seeds for State 0 and unsticking
- Character lies: "Power is the only protection", "I can only rely on myself", "If I'm perfect, I'll be loved", "Asking for help is failure", "I must control everything", "Other people always leave".
- Ghosts that produce lies: parental abandonment, public humiliation, betrayal by a trusted figure, failure at a crucial moment, survivor's guilt, a broken promise with consequences, being complicit in harm, escaping while others did not.
- Scene-ending disasters: "Yes, but now a worse problem", "No, and the door is closed", "Yes, but it cost too much", "No, and you're exposed", "Yes, but trust is broken", "No, and your ally turns".
- Dilemma shapes: save the friend or finish the mission; tell the truth or protect someone; principle or pragmatism; mercy or justice; honesty or kindness; the individual or the many.
- Unexpected professions to break cliche: elevator inspector, medical illustrator, crime scene cleaner, patent examiner, foley artist, crossword constructor, sleep study technician. Unexpected places: toll booth at 3 AM, closed water park, hospital chapel, self-storage at midnight, weather research station.
- Scene openings: mid-argument that has clearly run for hours; two people pretending not to know each other; the aftermath of violence rather than the violence; the last normal moment before disruption; someone keeping composure while falling apart.

### Genre tension menus
- Mystery clue types: object in the wrong place, an alibi too perfect, a reaction that does not match the situation, a repeated "coincidence", the dog that did not bark.
- Thriller clocks: evidence being destroyed, a witness leaving the country, medicine running low, a cover story about to fail.
- Horror dread: reflections that are wrong, animals behaving oddly, the safe place is not, the thing knows their name, it is patient.
- Romance obstacles: competing for the same goal, a hidden past, class gap, one is the other's boss, bad timing, different futures, pride.

### Characters from story functions
- Build secondary characters from an abstract function first, then dress them in a setting-specific form. Functions: healer, enforcer, keeper of secrets, maker, trader, guide, entertainer, death worker, transgressor.
- Each function gives story access to something: healer to bodies, vulnerability and life-or-death decisions; enforcer to authority, violence and moral compromise. Example forms of enforcer: contemporary parole officer or health inspector; historical thief-taker or exciseman; fantasy oath keeper or border warden; science fiction quarantine enforcer or customs officer at a jump gate.

### Using the loop
- After one intervention, reassess before recommending a second. The state often shifts (a world that was thin becomes alive, and the real problem is now pacing).
- Weigh accuracy against energy: if the diagnosis points at the dull task but the writer lights up on another, start with what energizes them, then return.
- Keep asking "does this feel right?" beside every structural judgment; a story can satisfy every framework and still be dead.
