# economic-systems
## When to use
Use when a world needs currencies, trade networks, resource economies or labor structures, or when economic pressure should drive plot and character motivation instead of sitting in the background.

## Core rules
- Build from ten principles: resource foundation, exchange evolution (barter to abstract instruments), production organization, institutional embeddedness, technological determinism, scarcity-abundance spectrum, center-periphery dynamics, formal-informal balance, subsistence-surplus transition (surplus enables specialization), and cyclical patterns of growth, crisis and adaptation.
- Choose values across ten parameter categories. Resources: critical resource, distribution, renewability, energy source, land productivity. Production: labor organization (family, guild, wage, slave, automated), ownership, specialization, technology level, scale.
- Exchange: value representation, medium (barter, commodity, currency, credit, reputation), market structure, trade network shape, price determination (haggling, posted, auction, algorithmic). Distribution: wealth distribution, redistribution (tax, charity, inheritance, theft), access limits, basic needs provision, luxury allocation.
- Temporal: seasonality, growth expectations, investment horizon, generational transfer, crisis response. Organizational: decision-making, information flows, regulation, enforcement, institutional complexity. Socio-cultural: status-economy link, moral constraints (such as interest prohibitions), cultural valuation, occupation as identity, ritual exchange.
- Spatial: density, transport, urban-rural relation, regional specialization, border economics and smuggling. Knowledge: skill transmission, innovation patterns, information asymmetry, trade secrets and guild mysteries. Ecological: environmental impact, limits, sustainability, climate adaptation, disaster resilience.
- Pick a system type: subsistence (hunter-gatherer, pastoral, farming), traditional exchange (gift, prestige, ceremonial, redistributive chiefdom, local market), command (palace, feudal, state socialist, war, corporate), market (mercantile, industrial, financial, mixed, network), or alternative (commons, cooperative, post-scarcity, reputation, automated).
- Design the currency: name pattern (material-based, weight-based, authority-based, value-representing, historical), material, backing (full, partial, fiat, reputation), issuer (state, bank, guild, individual), portability, divisibility.
- Work in order: resource base, production, exchange, distribution, organization, culture integration, then system evolution with crisis points and technological transitions.
- Match financial complexity to institutional capacity: guild-controlled trade and reputation credit precede anonymous markets; a peasant village cannot have a derivatives market.
- Model trade friction: high-value, low-weight goods travel far, bulky staples stay local; count transport cost, spoilage, banditry, tolls and political barriers.
- Keep supporting sectors even around a prestige export: farmers, transporters, administrators, entertainers. The key resource should dominate politics, not erase other economics.
- Give currency failure modes: counterfeiting, debasement, trust crises, competing currencies, and what happens when the issuer's authority is questioned.
- Account for labor at every level, and design the shadow economy: black markets and informal trade.
- Minimum deliverables: key resources and distribution, system type, currency, trade routes, labor organization, wealth distribution, at least one historical economic crisis, shadow economy, link to the status system, and future pressures for plot.

## Diagnose
- What is the fundamental scarcity, and who controls it?
- Who issues the money, what backs it, and what would break trust in it?
- Where does food come from, and who does the basic work behind elite luxury?
- What moves along each trade route, and what does it cost to move it?
- Does the financial complexity match the era's information systems, law and enforcement?
- What is illegal or informal, and who profits from it?
- Does the economy depend on one commodity with nothing else supporting it?
- When was the last crisis, and how did the economy adapt?
- Where does economic role create status, identity or conflict for the characters?

## How to apply
Reply in the language of the request. Return a paste-ready economy sheet with these headings: Summary (system type and one-line logic); Resources (critical resources, distribution, renewability); Production and Labor (ownership, labor forms, specialization, tech); Currency (name, material, backing, issuer, divisibility, a failure scenario); Trade (three to five routes with goods, friction and chokepoints); Distribution (classes, wealth pattern, redistribution, access limits); Institutions and Regulation (who decides, who enforces, record-keeping); Culture Link (status, moral limits, ritual exchange); Shadow Economy (three concrete examples); History and Pressures (one past crisis, two current pressures usable as plot). Mark invented elements as proposals, keep consistent with the user's lore, and flag any detail that is anachronistic for the world's technology.


## Deep notes

Option lists to pick from when a parameter is still blank:
- Critical resource: food, water, energy, materials, information, magic. Distribution: concentrated, dispersed, layered, mobile. Energy: biological, fossil, renewable, magical, stellar. Land: high yield, marginal, specialized, variable.
- Ownership: private, communal, state, corporate, mixed. Specialization: generalist, craft, industrial, hyper-specialized. Scale: household, workshop, factory, planetary.
- Value representation: intrinsic, symbolic, credit, reputation. Market structure: gift, local, regional, global, virtual. Trade network shape: point-to-point, hub-and-spoke, mesh, guild-controlled.
- Wealth distribution: egalitarian, stratified, winner-take-all. Access: open, credentialed, hereditary, geographic. Basic needs provided by market, state, community or family. Luxuries allocated by purchase, status, lottery or merit.
- Growth expectation: growth-oriented, steady-state, cyclical, declining. Investment horizon: short-term, generational, eternal. Generational transfer: inheritance, merit, dissolution, communal.
- Decision-making: central planning, market, consensus, hierarchy. Information: open, restricted, asymmetric, broadcast. Regulation: minimal, comprehensive, guild-based, religious. Enforcement: state, community, market, magical.
- Status relation: wealth equals status, status separate from wealth, or status inverted (the poor are holy). Occupation as identity: fixed, separate, fluid.
- Transport: foot, animal, mechanical, teleportation. Urban-rural: integrated, exploitative, separate. Border economics: free flow, controlled, smuggling.
- Skill transmission: apprenticeship, formal education, inherited. Innovation source: individual, institutional, imported, divine. Information asymmetry: transparent, insider knowledge, secret.
- Environmental impact: sustainable, extractive, restorative. Disaster resilience: robust, fragile, adaptive.

Type details. A gift economy runs on relationship and obligation, so the debt itself is the bond. A prestige economy distributes goods to win status. A redistributive chiefdom collects at the center and hands back, so the storehouse is the political heart. A palace or temple economy centers production on a royal or sacred institution. A feudal system pays in land-based service obligations. A war economy points all production at the military. Corporate command is the company town or plantation, where the employer is also landlord and shopkeeper. Mercantile capitalism is trade-focused with state direction; industrial is production-focused; financial is dominated by investment and credit; network capitalism runs on flexible specialization. Commons-based systems manage shared resources collectively; reputation economies allocate by standing; automated production is run by machines.

Currency naming patterns: material-based (a coin named for its silver), weight-based (pound, shekel, talent), authority-based (sovereign, crown, imperial), value-representing (credit, note, token), historical reference (an old coin name kept after the coin changed). Name drift is a free history lesson: a "pound" that no longer weighs a pound tells the reader about debasement.

Setting adaptations:
- Fantasy: control of arcane materials; guilds guarding specialized knowledge; religiously influenced production; trade between races; markets for ancient artifacts; dangerous harvesting of monster parts, which prices those goods by the risk to the harvester.
- Science fiction: post-scarcity automation (scarcity then moves to non-replicable goods, attention and status); faster-than-light commerce; closed-system resource management in habitats; alien market integration; minds as a resource; economies shaped by extreme lifespans.
- Post-apocalyptic: salvage economies; water and food as the base of value; lost information as a tradable good; isolated enclaves that still manufacture.

Case studies, with the lesson from each:
- A single spice that enables interstellar travel gives its monopolist extreme leverage, and the religious-economic guild around it shows how a critical resource fuses with faith; the desert ecology also limits how production can work.
- A replicator-based post-scarcity society shows status and achievement replacing wealth as motivation, yet non-replicable goods stay scarce, and the border with money-using societies is where the friction and stories live.
- A world with multi-year winters forces long storage cycles; a banking house can hold power over crowns; regions specialize (a wine, a lace); free and slave labor compete.

Worked check for frictionless trade. If wool moves four hundred miles by ox cart, ask what it costs per mile, how much spoils or is stolen, how many tolls it pays, and who profits at each stop. Typical result: the wool only travels that far if it is dyed or woven first, which raises value per weight; grain stays within a few days of the river or sea. Use this to place workshops and markets.

Step prompts per layer:
- Resources: map key resources and who holds them, energy limits, the renewable balance, extraction technology, and the power relationships resources create.
- Production: primary methods, ownership and control, labor structures, production tools, occupational specialization.
- Exchange: the currency or value form, market structures and locations, routes, price mechanisms, transaction customs.
- Distribution: accumulation patterns, how much inequality exists and how it is justified, redistribution, access limits, luxury versus necessity.
- Organization: decision institutions, information flow, regulation, enforcement, record-keeping.
- Culture: economic role tied to status, moral limits, economically significant rituals, economic identity markers.
- Evolution: historical stages, crisis points and adaptations, the effect of technological change, the balance of formal and informal sectors.

Extra failure signs: a technology introduced with no economic effect (trace what it makes cheap, what it ruins, and who loses a job); a static economy with no cycles; an economy that only appears as backdrop and never touches a character's choices; modern financial concepts in a pre-industrial world.
