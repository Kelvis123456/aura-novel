# Long-form hooks

## When to use
Use when designing or fixing the first three chapters, a chapter opening, a chapter ending, a paragraph-level micro-hook, or any suspense and cliffhanger structure in a serialized, multi-chapter story.

## Core rules
- Start where the conflict is hottest. The protagonist must appear within about 300 words, and the first payoff or point of anticipation must land within about 1,000 words.
- Put the protagonist in one of three states on page one: in danger, enviable, or dropped into an unfamiliar place. Chapter one must make clear the protagonist's goal and what the book sells.
- Do not open with weather, scenery, long backstory, a prologue, a dream, a flashback, or world-building lectures. Never introduce three or more major characters at once; name only characters who matter later.
- Complete three anchors in the first three chapters: persona (who the protagonist is and what hurts), entry point (the first conflict or opportunity, ideally in chapter one), and edge (the protagonist's unique advantage). Distressed protagonists lead with persona; advantaged ones lead with the edge.
- Make the first conflict consequential: state what is gained if it is solved and lost if it is not. Trigger any special power through that crisis, never by casual announcement.
- Release background in small lumps ranked by priority: danger, then persona, then a hint of the edge, then world. Fold exposition into conflict, for example bystanders arguing.
- Write the chapter ending in about 100 words: point at the problem and stop. Every chapter ends with an unanswered question or unmet expectation. Quiet chapters may use a weak hook, such as an emotional pull or a stated next goal.
- Never use the same hook type in two consecutive chapters; never run one type for three or more. Hook strength follows story stage: strong in chapter one, strong before climaxes, medium in everyday stretches, closing in the finale.
- Chapter-ending toolkit: sudden reveal, urgent crisis, interrupted action, identity twist, dilemma between two bad options, mystery object, countdown, promise or threat, impossible disappearance, hidden meaning, image that changes, echo of the opening line with one detail altered, and restraint (show only the reaction, withhold the cause).
- Chapter-opening toolkit: suspenseful dialogue mid-conversation, flash-forward fragment, countdown opening, eerie first-person monologue, contrasting scenes side by side, interrupted action, imagery that foreshadows.
- Match the ending to the chapter's mood: mystery wants reveal or restraint, crisis wants countdown or unfinished action, twists want identity or hidden meaning, relationship chapters want dilemmas or emotional turns.
- Use the three-stage chapter hook: plant in the first 30%, grow in the middle 50%, then detonate or delay detonation in the last 20%. A delayed detonation is stronger than a detonation because it pulls into the next chapter.
- Suspense exists when readers see two or more plausible outcomes. Suspense is a ticking timer, surprise is a sudden blast; prefer the timer. A time lock early in the story compresses tension.
- Grade every chapter on a five-step scale: curiosity, want the next scene, want the next chapter, cannot put it down, cannot sleep. Transitions need level 1, regular chapters level 2, key chapters level 3 or more.
- Run several suspense arcs at once: short (2-3 chapters), medium (5-8), long (a whole volume). Keep at least two running at every moment, and always hold readers' curiosity on two long things and one short thing.
- Never close an expectation without a new one already open. When the protagonist is about to receive something, interpose another hook before delivery.
- Order information deliberately: question then answer; question, honest clue, answer; question, false clue, answer; question, two conflicting false clues, answer. False clues must be believable.
- A reaction validates every suspense beat. A twist or reveal with no character reacting falls flat. Stack astonishment in layers: one person, then a network of people, then escalating achievements; prefer reactors who matter later, and show it through objects changing.
- Micro-hooks inside paragraphs: information gap (reader knows, character does not), countdown, reversal, held card, imminent comeuppance, cost, a vulnerable person, a cold discovery. Keep information gaps under about 3,000 words and always pay off a countdown.
- Show conflict through escalating dialogue: stating facts, adding suggestions, accusing, accusing plus commanding, accusing plus belittling disguised as concern. Make harm unfair, not merely painful, and prefer others doubting the protagonist and being proven wrong to the protagonist truly suffering.
- Anticipation outranks payoff. The more setup, the bigger the release; build readers' wanting before delivering, and let each release exceed the previous one.
- Avoid false suspense, deus ex machina openings, endless withholding, low-stakes hooks, and repeating one hook type.

## Diagnose
- Does the conflict or danger appear in the first 100 words?
- Is the protagonist on stage and wanting something within 300 words?
- Can the reader state what the protagonist wants and what threatens it by the end of chapter one?
- Does the chapter end on an open question, and is the threat real rather than instantly dissolved?
- Did this chapter reuse the previous chapter's hook type?
- Does every suspense beat have a visible character reaction?
- Is any hook planted but never paid off?
- Are at least two expectation lines running right now?
- Is any twist introduced without prior clues?

## How to apply
Reply in the language of the request. For an opening: choose the strategy (crisis, mystery, or reversal), the three anchors, and a beat plan with word budgets; then write or outline it. For a chapter ending or opening: diagnose the chapter's mood and story stage, choose one hook type from the toolkit, state why, and give the revised passage of roughly 100 words. For a suspense plan: list the short, medium, and long arcs, the information-order template for each key scene, and the expectation handoff between them. Name the technique used and keep explanations short.

## Deep notes
Golden opening, step by step. First, list every event available for the opening and start with the one carrying the most dramatic tension. Second, state the protagonist's state (danger, enviable, or displaced). Third, deliver the three anchors. For a distressed protagonist use this sequence: unfavorable situation (empathy), pressure or crisis (urgency), edge activated (hope), partial solution that creates a bigger problem (hook). For an advantaged protagonist: hidden advantage underestimated, accidental exposure, bystanders' beliefs rewritten, attention or trouble attracted. A workable word budget for the first stretch: 300 words of situation, 500 for the first small drama, 400 for the edge giving safety, 400 for a new threat, 500 for the crisis, 400 for using the edge to resolve it.

Weak versus strong ending. Weak: "He decided to sleep, tomorrow would be a big day." Strong: "He folded the letter into his pocket and said nothing. The date on it was a week after his own funeral." The strong version carries a revelation and a reaction. Weak opening: "The sun rose over the quiet village." Strong: "Seventy-two hours until the contract expired, and nobody in the house knew yet that the signature was forged."

Ending templates. Edge activation: lowest point, one sensory detail, the system or power switching on. Foreshadowed reversal: the protagonist watches the opponent gloat coldly, then one short line promising they will not laugh after today, details withheld. Cutoff: a small action, a violent reaction, a question, no answer. Choice: two options, each with a tempting reward and a hidden cost, no decision shown. Time jump: a precise time marker, an unexpected result, a line implying the process. Dialogue turn: a request, a reply, and a second half the reader did not expect.

Layered trigger hook inside one scene. Layer one: show the first result and the audience's first reaction. Layer two: reveal it is not the final result and raise expectation. Layer three: show something beyond expectation and make onlookers gasp. Layer four: hint the protagonist can go further, and open the next stretch. Prop-based escalation makes this concrete: an opponent's chair handle cracks, then the chair is full of cracks, then the handle is crushed.

Expectation relay for items and abilities, eight steps: show the item is strong; a side character calls it junk because of missing information; show an enemy it counters; a side character with better gear fails; the protagonist makes a targeted plan; onlookers doubt; the protagonist wins and everyone is stunned; a new gain and a new expectation appear.

Five-phase driver for any stretch: create a want through injustice or lack, give hope through the edge, make solving it cost effort in layers or escalating problems, then grant the reward. Keep the emotional line moving up and down: setback sizes small, never caused by the protagonist's own error, and always followed by a larger recovery.

Failure modes: ending every chapter with the same shout; suspense without a reaction; false clues that serve no plot function; revealing everything to be nothing; stuffing in so many hooks that none can be paid. Fix by cutting to one strong hook per chapter, and paying one older hook every few chapters.

Paragraph-level combinations that work: information gap plus held card (readers know the protagonist is saving a move), countdown plus cost (time pressure and every choice loses something), reversal plus comeuppance (a mood swing in one beat), vulnerable person plus cost (maximum sympathy). Strong single beats: an out-of-place object that changes a scene, or a calm reaction to a shocking discovery, where the calm itself creates suspense. Keep a held card in short fiction to about a third of the length at most, and keep a reversal fair with a clue planted earlier.

Unfair harm, built carefully. Harm lands harder when the protagonist is innocent and the injury is mental as much as physical. Three common patterns: a villain shifting their own cost onto the protagonist and acting entitled, a villain pushing blame onto the protagonist in a quiet, scheming way, and a villain whose order was disrupted by the protagonist and who leaves no way out. Everyone suffering a cut is fair; only the protagonist getting a lesser share is unfair, and that is what stirs readers.

Onlooker quality. Extras give pure reaction with no consequence. Informed professionals compare and change their attitude. Top-level figures react with information, relationship movement, and fresh expectation. Prefer many kinds of onlookers, many people, many ranks, and people the protagonist already knows, because an acquaintance's shock is worth more than a stranger's.

Pre-delivery checklist for any chapter ending: Is there an open question? Is the threat concrete and costly? Is it different from the last two endings? Does the next chapter's first lines answer it honestly instead of through luck? If the answer to the last question is no, rewrite the ending, not the next chapter.
