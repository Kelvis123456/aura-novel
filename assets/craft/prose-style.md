# prose-style
## When to use
Use when the structure is solid but the sentences are flat, monotonous, vague, overwritten, passive, or the narrator's voice wobbles. It is last-mile sentence-level polish, not a fix for plot or scene problems.

## Core rules
- Treat style as content: the way something is written shapes what it means. Vigorous writing is concise, and every word earns its place.
- Make prose invisible when it works. The reader should experience the story, not notice the writing. Serve this story, this character, this moment: sometimes sparse, sometimes rich, always intentional.
- Vary sentence length. Short sentences give punch and urgency (action, revelation), medium sentences give clarity and flow (the default), long sentences give development and immersion.
- Vary sentence structure (simple, compound, complex) and sentence openings. Do not always start with subject-verb; open with modifiers or dependent clauses sometimes.
- Vary paragraph length too, including single-line paragraphs for punch, and shift rhythm between sections.
- Use the punch position: the end of a sentence carries the most weight. Put the emphatic word last. "I remember: it was a dark night" beats "It was a dark night, I remember."
- Prefer concrete over vague abstraction (laughter, unlocked door, kiss, clock). Abstraction is fine when precise: "the particular happiness of a child with a new dog."
- Replace generic words with specific ones. Let strong nouns and verbs do the work instead of modifiers.
- Limit modifiers: no more than 1-2 adjectives per noun, adverbs sparingly. Choose one right adjective, not three approximations ("beautiful, gorgeous, stunning sunset"; "ran quickly, desperately, frantically").
- Metaphors must illuminate, not obscure. Replace overwrought or mixed metaphors with simpler images. Do not use elevated register for mundane content.
- Default to active voice. Passive is right when the agent is unimportant or unknown, when hiding the actor on purpose, when emphasizing the sentence end, or when the receiver is the focus ("Kennedy was assassinated").
- Make pronoun antecedents obvious, show logical steps instead of compressing them, and give context for specialized terms (the curse of knowledge).
- Use the right word even if repeated. Avoid thesaurus abuse, elegant variation (different words for the same thing), and jargon where plain words fit.
- Keep parallel ideas in parallel form ("reading, swimming, and running"), keep related words together, put statements in positive form, omit needless words, avoid strings of loose sentences.
- Keep one baseline voice: a consistent diction level (high/formal, middle/standard, low/informal), tone, and distance from the reader. Depart from the baseline only on purpose, driven by character or scene.
- Treat the rules as principles, not laws. Do not call rich prose purple automatically: rich serves the story, purple overwhelms it. Break a rule only intentionally.

## Diagnose
- Do sentence lengths, structures, and openings vary, or does it drone?
- Are the word choices specific or generic?
- Are there adjective or adverb stacks, or metaphors that obscure?
- Does the style call attention to itself instead of the story?
- Is passive voice used by default, hiding agents who matter?
- Would a reader have to reread any sentence? Are there vague pronouns or compressed logic?
- Does the diction level or tone shift without a reason?
- Does the end of each sentence land on the strongest word?
- Read it aloud: where would a reader stumble, run out of breath, or hit a repeated word?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give a short diagnosis: name the main problem state (flat, unclear, overwrought, monotonous, passive, or inconsistent voice), quote the specific phrases that show it, and note any read-aloud stumbles. Keep it to a few lines. Then give a rewritten passage that fixes those problems while keeping the plot content, point of view, and the author's baseline voice. Do not add new events or invent details. Where the original already works, leave it alone. If rhythm is the issue, change length and structure; if wordiness, cut; if vagueness, make it concrete. End with one line saying which techniques you applied.


## Deep notes
Name the problem state before touching a sentence, because each state has a different cure.
- Flat: the sentences deliver information but have no rhythm and nothing distinctive. Cure with variety and specific words.
- Unclear: the reader rereads. Cure with concrete language, visible logic, and checked pronouns.
- Overwrought: style outweighs substance. Cure by cutting modifiers and simplifying images.
- Monotonous: every sentence and paragraph has the same shape. Cure with deliberate variation.
- Passive by default: agents are hidden and energy drains. Cure by converting default passives.
- Inconsistent voice: diction drifts and chapters sound like different writers. Cure by fixing a baseline.

Rough numeric guardrails for a scene or chapter. Treat them as smoke alarms, not laws.
- Passive constructions above about 20 percent of sentences signal default passivity. Action sequences should sit well below that.
- Forms of be, have, do, get, make, seem, appear, become above about 15 percent of words signal weak verbs. Look for the real verb hiding in a noun.
- Adverbs above about 4 percent of words signal modifier dependence, especially "-ly" words stacked on speech or movement.
- More than a few adjective stacks in a passage means the writer is hoping something sticks.
- Filter words (saw, heard, felt, noticed, realized, thought, knew, watched, wondered, decided) in more than about one sentence in ten put a pane of glass between reader and experience. Cut the filter and give the perception directly: "She saw the door was open" becomes "The door was open."

Sentence length bands for auditing rhythm. Under 10 words is short, 10 to 20 is medium, 21 to 35 is long, over 35 is very long. A healthy page uses at least three of the four bands. Count the first word of each sentence across a page: if one opener (he, she, the, it) begins a third of them, restructure. Count sentences per paragraph too: a page of nothing but three-to-five-sentence blocks drones as surely as a page of identical sentences. A paragraph of one or two sentences is a deliberate gear change, so spend it where something lands.

Before and after patterns.
- Stacked modifiers: "She walked quickly, nervously, and anxiously down the long, dark, narrow corridor." Pick the one that carries the beat and let the verb do the rest: "She hurried down the corridor, counting doors."
- Weak verb plus noun: "He made a decision to leave." Becomes "He decided to leave." Better still, show the act: "He picked up his coat."
- Buried emphasis: "The letter arrived too late, unfortunately." Put the weight last: "Unfortunately, too late: the letter arrived." Or simply end on the fact that hurts: "The letter arrived the day after the funeral."
- Compressed logic: "The bridge was out; they took the river road." Show the missing step if the reader cannot supply it: "The bridge was out, so they took the river road, which meant passing the mill."
- Vague pronoun: "Mara told Ines she had been wrong." Name the one who was wrong, or restructure so only one referent is possible.
- Mixed metaphor: "The silence was a blanket that crackled with knives." Pick one image and commit to it.
- Elevated register for mundane content: "He ingested the beverage" for a man drinking coffee. Match the weight of the language to the weight of the moment, unless the mismatch is the joke.
- Loose-sentence chain: a run of clauses joined by "and" or "then." Break it, subordinate the minor beats, and let one clause stand as the main event.

Word choice rules of thumb. Use the plain word when the plain word is exact. Repeat a noun rather than cycle through "the man, the figure, the stranger, the individual" for one person; the reader tracks identity and mistakes the new label for a new character. Reserve a rare word for the one place its precision is needed, so it registers. Place related words next to each other: "He only wanted one thing" and "He wanted only one thing" mean different things, and the position of "only" is your decision, not an accident.

Voice baseline checklist. Before revising a long passage, write down the narrator's diction level, typical sentence length, distance from the character (close or removed), and tone. Revise toward that baseline. Flag intrusive author commentary in close point of view: a narrator who knows more words than the character should be seen as a choice, not a slip. A deliberate shift (a panicked character's syntax fracturing) should be traceable to the scene, not to the writer's mood.

Common failure modes in revision. Over-correcting into uniformity: cutting every adverb and passive until the prose is clean and lifeless. Replacing a flaw with a different flaw: swapping adjective stacks for a metaphor pile. Polishing a scene that will be cut. Calling rich prose purple by reflex when the writer's style is deliberately ornate and consistent. When the style is a conscious choice and it serves the story, leave it.
