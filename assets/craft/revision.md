# revision
## When to use
Use when revision feels overwhelming, when changes cascade unpredictably, when the writer can no longer see problems, or when editing never ends.

## Core rules
- Revision is many activities at different scales. Work from largest to smallest: developmental (structure, story), then line (sentences, paragraphs), then copy (mechanics, consistency). Polishing prose in a scene you will later cut is wasted effort.
- Use six focused passes in order: Structural, Scene, Character, Dialogue, Prose, Polish. One type of problem per pass; focus enables seeing and prevents cascade waste.
- Structural pass: audit each scene for the POV character's goal, the conflict, the disaster or outcome, whether it advances plot or character, and whether the story survives without it. Decide keep, cut, combine or revise. Verify the arc: the lie believed at the start, the truth learned, the transformation moments, and whether the climax requires the transformation. Map peaks and valleys and check that tension escalates.
- Scene pass: does the scene start late enough and end early enough, is the transition clear, is the scene-sequel balance right. Necessity test: could it be cut, combined, or summarized instead of dramatized?
- Character pass: read one character's dialogue alone for distinct voice; cover tags and check who speaks; check motivation for each major action and whether changes are earned; check arc progress is visible in behavior.
- Dialogue pass: subtext beneath the surface (characters saying exactly what they mean is usually bad), tension between speakers, and each exchange advancing plot, revealing character or building relationships, ideally several.
- Prose pass: passive voice unless intentional, weak verbs (is, was, had, made, got), filter words (saw, heard, felt, noticed), adverb overuse, redundancy, unclear pronouns; vary paragraph length, make opening sentences work; integrate description with action and use specific details.
- Polish pass: spelling (especially names), grammar, tense, punctuation, formatting, and consistency of names, timeline, physical descriptions and world rules. Read aloud and in a different format.
- Define "done" for each pass and limit the number of rounds. Revision ends when each pass finds less than the one before; accept "good enough".
- Gain distance first: days or weeks away, change format, read aloud.
- Feedback: gather all of it before acting; repeated notes on the same area are a real problem, a single reader's taste is preference; look for the underlying issue conflicting readers both sensed; implement systematically, not reactively. Beta readers after the structural pass, critique partners after prose, proofreaders after polish.
- Keep cuts in a "darlings" folder. Focus on strengthening what remains.
- Never revise immediately after drafting.

## Diagnose
- Is the structure sound, and has it been checked before any prose work?
- Which scenes could be cut, combined or summarized?
- Does the protagonist's arc complete, and does the climax require it?
- Where are the peaks and valleys, and does tension escalate?
- Do the characters sound distinct, and are motivations consistent?
- Does dialogue carry subtext and do work?
- Which sentences have weak verbs, filter words or redundancy?
- Is the writer polishing at the wrong level?
- Are the problems found real, or manufactured by perfectionism?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give a diagnosis: say which level the passage needs (developmental, line or copy), list the top three to five problems in priority order, and note what already works. If structure is in doubt, say so before touching sentences. Then return a revised passage that addresses only the level diagnosed: for structure, cut, combine or reorder scenes and state what changed; for line work, tighten sentences, replace weak verbs, remove filter words and redundancy; for copy, correct mechanics. Preserve the author's voice, events and meaning. Mark each substantial cut in a short list so it can be saved. Finish with the next pass the writer should do.

## Deep notes
Revision is construction, not repair: the draft was exploration, and revision builds the final manuscript. The recurring failure is working at the wrong level, so name the level before touching text.

What each level hunts for:
- Developmental: plot holes and logic failures, missing or weak motivation, scenes that advance neither plot nor character, pacing too slow or too rushed, unclear or absent theme, weak opening or ending, inconsistent characterization. Done when the story works structurally and major changes are finished.
- Line: awkward sentences, passive overuse, redundancy, unclear antecedents, dialogue that all sounds alike, description that overwhelms action, telling where showing is needed, weak verbs and vague nouns. Done when the prose is clean at sentence level.
- Copy: spelling, grammar, punctuation, capitalization, timeline slips, name variants, formatting, and facts if relevant. Done when the manuscript is clean and consistent.

Why multiple passes work: looking for one thing reveals what scanning for everything misses; structural changes invalidate line work; each pass limits decision load; completed passes are visible progress. 

The six states, with their checks:
- R1 Overwhelmed: too many visible problems and no priority. Confirm structure is evaluated before prose, major before minor, one pass at a time.
- R2 Blind: you cannot see problems any more. Wait days or weeks, switch format dramatically (print if you read on screen), read aloud because the ear catches what the eye misses, and bring in outside readers.
- R3 Endless: ask what you are finding on pass fifteen that you did not find on pass three. Set a limit such as three full passes maximum, define done for each pass, separate real problems from manufactured ones, and stop when marginal returns diminish.
- R4 Conflicted: one reader says pacing is too fast, another too slow. Ask where each comment applies; the underlying issue may be uneven pacing, not uniform speed. A pattern is a real problem; one reader's stylistic taste is your call.
- R5 Delete-phobic: ask whether you are attached to the writing or to the story. Cut material served the draft by helping you find the story. Keep a darlings folder so nothing is deleted, only moved.
- R6 Wrong level: stop prose work the moment structure is uncertain, confirm a scene stays before polishing it, then work top-down: structure, scenes, lines, copy.

Pass questions to run:
- Structural: Is there a clear dramatic question? Is the protagonist's goal clear from the start? Does the protagonist drive the resolution? Is the climax the highest tension point? Does the ending emerge from the story? Do all major arcs complete?
- Scene: Does each scene start late enough and end early enough, with no excessive setup or wrap-up drag? Which scenes can be combined for efficiency? In the audit, mark every scene keep, cut, combine or revise, and track words per scene to spot outliers.
- Character: Is the lie clear at the start, the truth learned by the end, and the transformation visible in choices? Are setbacks and advances balanced? Can you tell who speaks with tags covered?
- Dialogue: Is dialogue free of exposition dumps? Are tags unobtrusive? Is there tension between speakers? Read one character's lines aloud in sequence.
- Prose: Vary sentence length and structure as well as paragraph length. Check that description is proportional to importance, uses specific detail, and engages more than one sense. Check that openings do work and transitions are present but not heavy-handed.
- Polish: Check tense consistency, subject-verb agreement, dialogue formatting, chapter and scene breaks, place names, physical descriptions and world rules. Do the final read aloud, on a different device or format, at a slower speed.

Feedback timing and handling:
- Beta readers after the structural pass (story-level problems and engagement). Critique partners and representation-accuracy readers after the prose pass. Proofreaders after polish.
- Process: gather everything first; look for patterns; separate preference from problem; prioritize by impact; implement systematically.

Worked example. A writer reports that a thriller feels both exhausting and slow. Diagnosis at the developmental level: the middle has seven consecutive scenes ending in the same kind of escape, and two scenes restate a clue. Decisions: combine the two clue scenes into one, cut one escape and save it in the darlings folder, and move the revelation about the informant earlier. Only then, in the prose pass, trim filter words ("she noticed that the door was open" becomes "the door was open"). Doing the second step first would have polished a scene that was cut.

Anti-patterns and fixes:
- Endless Polisher: define pass goals, set limits, accept good enough.
- Bottom-Up Editor: structure first.
- Feedback Slave: implementing everything loses authorial vision and builds a patchwork; look for patterns.
- Delete-Phobic: save cuts, strengthen what remains.

Workflow order: rest period, structural pass, optional beta readers, scene, character, dialogue and prose passes, optional critique partners, polish, optional proofreader, done.
