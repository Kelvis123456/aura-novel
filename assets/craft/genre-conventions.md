# genre-conventions
## When to use
Use when the genre promise is unclear, genre elements feel misplaced or missing, or a secondary genre competes with the primary. It checks that the story delivers the emotional experience it promises.

## Core rules
- Genre is a promise: the story must deliver it or readers feel betrayed. Genre is the emotional experience, not the bookshelf category or setting. Science fiction, fantasy, historical and contemporary are settings; a fantasy can be a mystery, romance or thriller.
- The eleven elemental genres and their promises: Wonder (awe, "I had no idea that was possible"), Idea (intellectual stimulation), Adventure (excitement through challenges, external "what happens next?"), Horror (dread, confrontation with threat), Mystery (curiosity about unknown facts), Thriller (tension through danger, "will they make it in time?"), Humor (amusement, unexpected and delightful), Relationship (investment in connection), Drama (internal conflict and transformation), Issue (exploring complex questions), Ensemble (group dynamics, "how will they come together?").
- Signal the genre in the first chapter: the opening scene needs at least one clear marker of the intended experience. Test: can you answer "what kind of story is this?" in one word?
- Pick the genre from the material, not the reverse. Thriller pacing on relationship content, or mystery structure with an adventure payoff, means material and genre are fighting.
- Place elements at the right time. Clues must be distributed for progressive revelation, not dumped; thriller tension needs stakes first and must escalate; horror dread needs vulnerability set up first; relationship obstacles first block, then release; wonder builds through discovery.
- Required elements per genre. Wonder: vast scale, discovery, a perspective shift. Idea: concept exploration and hypothesis testing. Adventure: varied environments and progressive challenges. Horror: isolation, vulnerabilities matching the threat, something to lose, escalating threat and diminishing safety. Mystery: fair-play clues, investigators, suspects, progressive revelation. Thriller: a deadline or ticking clock, matched antagonists, escalating threats. Humor: blind spots, miscommunication, subverted expectations. Relationship: forced proximity, complementary traits, meaningful obstacles, connection tests. Drama: values facing tests and difficult choices. Issue: diverse perspectives on the central question. Ensemble: complementary abilities and cooperation challenges.
- Keep a genre hierarchy: one primary genre dominates; secondary genres deepen it and are contained in specific scenes or subplots. Cut a secondary genre if it persistently undermines the primary.
- Do not let setting do the genre's work. Remove the setting mentally; if no emotional genre remains, the story lacks one.
- Elements must reinforce one unified experience; a checklist of inert clues, time pressure and dread is not a genre.
- Do not bait-and-switch: if the story is a hybrid, show both elements early.
- Deliver the expected emotion by unexpected means: subvert execution, not the contract. A story that subverts every expectation gives readers no stable ground.
- Ensemble or multi-POV: set an umbrella genre, give each thread a compatible subgenre, and keep tonal transitions coherent.
- Stale conventions: audit which are used by default and find the orthogonal version.

## Diagnose
- What emotional experience should the reader have, and does the opening promise it?
- Is the material actually serving a different genre than the one attempted?
- Are the required elements for this genre present, and in the right order?
- Is the tension, dread, mystery or romance escalating or static?
- Is a secondary genre serving the primary or competing with it?
- If the setting were removed, what genre would remain?
- Which conventions are being used by default, and which could be delivered more freshly?
- In a multi-thread story, do the threads combine into one experience?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give a diagnosis: the primary and any secondary genre you detect, the promise the passage makes, which required elements are present or missing, and the main problem (missing promise, wrong genre, misplaced elements, competing secondary genre, setting mistaken for genre, stale convention). Name one change that matters most. Then return a rewritten passage that fixes it, for instance by adding a genre marker to the opening, moving a clue or beat to its right place, adding a missing element such as a ticking clock or a vulnerability, or containing a competing subplot. Keep the author's plot, names, voice and chosen genre unless the author asked to change it.
