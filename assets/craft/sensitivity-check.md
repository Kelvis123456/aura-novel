# sensitivity-check
## When to use
An optional representation-craft tool the author chooses to run when writing characters or cultures beyond their own experience, or sensitive subject matter. It helps avoid flat stereotypes and improve authenticity; it never limits what may be written.

## Core rules
- Frame everything as craft advice. The author decides; the question is never "can I write this?" but "am I writing this well, with awareness of how it might land?"
- Be evaluative, informative, collaborative, specific and constructive: point to concrete passages, explain the craft reason, offer alternatives. Never prescribe, censor, demand removal, or claim to be the final word.
- Difficult, dark and uncomfortable material is legitimate fiction. Depicting is not endorsing; historical fiction may show historical attitudes.
- Cultural detail: give accurate context, give characters from the culture agency and voice, show diversity inside the culture, and avoid reducing practices to exotic decoration, outsider-explains-the-culture framing, or sacred practices used as aesthetics. Use language accurately, not for flavor.
- Gender: give all characters complex motivations and inner lives; balance physical description across genders; ask whose gaze narrates; avoid women who exist only to develop male characters, "not like other girls" framing, and violence used as a shortcut for character depth.
- Disability, mental health and body size: give characters identities beyond their condition; frame barriers as environmental as well as medical; let them thrive without a required cure; avoid inspiration-as-purpose, magical compensation, mental illness as explanation for violence, and fatness as comedy or moral failing.
- Stereotypes: characterize individuals, not representatives. Give marginalized characters the same range of traits as any other character, show several characters from one group so they differ, and subvert or examine a stereotype rather than playing it straight.
- Agency and voice: ask who drives the plot, whose perspective is centered, who makes meaningful choices. Avoid savior narratives, sidekick-only roles, and characters who exist to dispense wisdom to the protagonist.
- Tropes to handle knowingly: Bury Your Gays, fridging, Magical Negro, Mystical Native, Gay Best Friend, Manic Pixie Dream Girl, dangerous-queer coding, mentally ill equals violent. If using one, do it deliberately and visibly, or give the character a story beyond their suffering.
- Prefer quality over quantity: a few well-drawn characters beat many tokens. Absence of diversity in a setting that would plausibly contain it is also worth noticing.
- Judge words in context, not as isolated tokens. Do not apply purity tests or insist writers only write their own identity.
- Weigh observations as Critical, Significant, Minor or Note only as informational emphasis for the author; always say what the passage already does well.
- State limits: this is one perspective, communities are not monolithic, and it does not replace research or sensitivity readers with lived experience.

## Diagnose
- Which identities or cultures appear, and how is each characterized?
- Is any character defined mainly by a group trait rather than individual wants and quirks?
- Who makes the decisions that move the plot?
- Does a marginalized character exist mainly to help, teach or suffer for someone else?
- Are details specific and accurate, or generic and decorative?
- Does the physical description or gaze differ by gender or group?
- Is there variety among characters of the same group?
- If a known trope appears, is it chosen and examined or reproduced by default?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. Start with a brief, respectful diagnosis: what the passage does well, then up to four specific craft observations, each quoting the line, saying why it may read flat or stereotyped, and noting context. Make clear that the author's intent and final decision stand. Then offer a rewritten passage that deepens the character: add individual specificity, interiority, agency or accurate detail, while keeping the plot events, tone and darkness the author chose. Do not soften or remove the author's subject matter. If detail needs lived knowledge you cannot verify, say so and suggest research or a reader from that community instead of inventing facts.
