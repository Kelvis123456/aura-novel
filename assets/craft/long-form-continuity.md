# Long-Form Continuity

## When to use
Use for any manuscript that runs past a handful of chapters: tracking state, designing or auditing a cast, recalling material from earlier books or benchmark works, checking a chapter for consistency, or revising a chapter already written.

## Core rules
- Load only what the model would get wrong without: the current state of characters in this chapter, the foreshadowing due for payoff or advance, and the world rules the scene touches. Drop background with no causal link to the scene.
- Before each chapter, write a short scene brief with three parts: character states (one line each, including the latest change), relevant foreshadowing or prior history (with the chapter where it was planted), and world constraints.
- Keep a character state file for main characters only (three or more appearances or an own storyline): current identity, current ability, key relationships, public image, open foreshadowing, and a change log.
- After each chapter, update the state file only if identity, ability, relationship or public image changed. Add one log line. Keep the last 10 log lines per character and fold older ones into the summary fields.
- Keep three living trackers: foreshadowing (planted, advanced, paid off), timeline, and character states. Update them right after each chapter, not in batches.
- Past 30 chapters, summarize in layers: the last 5 chapters in detail, one paragraph per 10 chapters, one sentence per volume. Archive older detail every 50 chapters or at volume end, but never move active threads into the archive.
- Outline each chapter before writing it. Each chapter plan names its emotional target, hook, payoff, and the character relationship change from start to end.
- Give every important character three label layers: identity (what outsiders see), presentation (how they behave outwardly), core (who they really are). At least one layer must contrast with another.
- Show contrast through behavior, not narration. The reveal moment hits harder the wider the gap.
- Every character appearing needs a function (mentor, ally, information source, sacrifice, mirror, obstacle). A character with no function does not appear. Plan each minor character's exit.
- Limit speaking characters in a scene to three. Space out popular characters: reappear every 3 to 5 chapters with new information or a new function each time.
- Villains scale with their tier: small (1-5 chapters, one or two traits), medium (10-30 chapters, wins at least once), arc boss (own arc, ideological conflict, one scene that puts the hero in despair), final boss (foreshadowed from chapter one, overpowers the hero). Never make a villain stupid to hand the hero a win.
- Give every villain a motive that makes sense from his own view, and let obstacles come from his goals, not from an arbitrary wish to hurt the hero.
- Every important relationship gets at least one test (betrayal, sacrifice, misunderstanding) and an arc of change. A cast where everyone gets along is a flaw.
- Match romantic behavior to affection level, measured by the lower of the two parties. Intimate acts before the affection exists read as creepy or servile.
- When a plot need conflicts with a character's established personality, change the plot, not the character.
- In a series, name one main benchmark book. Take style and anchors only from it; take structure, character functions and setting ideas from secondary ones, within a budget. Never copy specific scenes.
- Revise in this order: locate the chapter, back up the original, decide full rewrite or local edit, then check cascade effects on foreshadowing, timeline, character states and following chapters.

## Diagnose
- If I removed this chapter, would anything downstream break?
- Does each character's behavior here match their established personality and current state?
- Which planted threads does this chapter advance or pay off, and which were forgotten?
- Does the chapter change the world, or only restate it?
- Is any supporting character functionless, or hogging the hero's spotlight?
- Does the villain act from a believable motive and keep the strength shown earlier?
- Do romantic or friendly behaviors outrun the current relationship stage?
- Did a rewrite change a fact that later chapters rely on?
- Is the original selling point still present every ten chapters or so?

## How to apply
When asked to check continuity of a chapter, answer in the language of the request. First list the facts the chapter asserts (character states, relationships, timeline, rules). Compare them with the supplied tracking notes and earlier text. Report each conflict with severity: critical (setting or motive contradiction without cause), high (timeline error, character data mismatch), medium (broken causal chain, forgotten secondary conflict), low (format). For each, give the exact fix. Then list threads that should be updated: new foreshadowing, payoffs, state changes.
When asked to update trackers, output the new rows only. When asked to revise a chapter, give the cascade list: later chapters that may need changes and why.

## Deep notes
State tracking template. Per character: current identity, current ability, key relationships (with the chapter of the last change), public image, open foreshadowing, change log. Scene brief for chapter N: Character states (one line each, include the most recent change). Relevant foreshadowing or history (plant details and chapter, and whether this chapter pays off or advances it). World constraints (only rules this scene touches). Example line: "Rivera: time traveler, formerly a mid-tier singer, just stunned a concert crowd, his fake girlfriend is beside him." Weak: a pasted full character sheet. Strong: three lines the writer would get wrong without.

Tracker fields. Foreshadowing: id, planted chapter, exact detail, status (open, advanced, paid), planned payoff chapter. Timeline: story date, event, characters present, chapter. Daily progress file: last finished chapter, date, words written, active foreshadowing count, most recent state changes, next chapter outline status, key decisions to remember.

Layered summaries. Recent five chapters: event, state change, foreshadowing. Every ten chapters: range, core events, state changes. Per volume: one line of main progress and key turn.

Character design framework. Protagonist: name, role in one sentence, identity tag, three to five appearance keywords, three to five personality keywords with a contradiction, core goal, emotional motive (revenge for a mother beats "become strongest"), fatal weakness that causes real mistakes in plot, signature phrase or gesture. Support: function, relation to hero, one or two traits, signature detail, exit. Motive chain: cause (specific: slapped in public, not "bullied"), intent, constraint (strong enough to stop a stroll to victory), risk (real). Motive conflict types: goal vs morality, emotion vs interest, short vs long term, self vs others.

Character methods. Strong, medium and weak ties: at least three story-driving traits (power, money, connections, background) per character; hobbies are weak ties only. A character is built around a soul or special trait, not stats. Repeat a behavior across scenes (behavior repetition point) so the reader knows the person. Obsession method for side characters: one obsession drives every habit; add one humanizing detail if extreme. Use a "white glove" aide who says and does what the hero should not, with a glow weaker than the hero's. A flat character can stay mysterious; flattening a strong one weakens it. Hero mistakes are forgivable if caused by force majeure or missing information, never by stupidity or saintliness. Heroes must not be saints, mindless fighters, evil at core, self-pitying, or foolish. If the hero's tone starts too high, press the momentum, not the person: leave a dimension where he is weak.

Relationships. Four types: conflict, alliance, intimate, authority. Four stages: stranger, acquaintance, confidant, lover. Allies at the confidant stage know each other's bottom line. Side characters warming to the hero need a buffer zone (distance, status gap, information gap) and five to seven turning points marked in the volume outline, each noting the attitude change. Side characters act on their own plans; they do not stand still waiting for the hero. Frenemies need high mutual regard plus absolute conflict. Romance: male-oriented lines display the partner's excellence; female-oriented lines deepen a unique connection. After the biggest obstacle, add no new conflict, only earned warmth. Rivalry scenes between love interests either tie back to the career line or give way to a shared outside threat.

Series recall. With several benchmark books, rank by relevance (same genre beats weak relevance), then by strength of reference, then user order. Budget: settings up to 2 per same-genre book and 8 total; outline up to 3 per book and 10 total; modules up to 2 per book and 8 total. Never read secondary books' style files or original text. Prose stage uses only the main benchmark style plus a budgeted structure summary. Output a recall table: book, strength, relevance, stage, count, how used.

Quality scoring. Five dimensions, 0 to 100 each: core consistency, surface originality, format consistency, readability, logical coherence. Repair strategy by weakest dimension: rewrite, compress, de-machine, or polish. Chapter checks: starts with a hook, advances something, ends on a change, no explanatory dump, no more than three new concepts per chapter, no filler (same emotion for three paragraphs, 500 words of scenery that does nothing, two chapters with no conflict). Opening three chapters: a hook in the first 500 words, hero present at once, event not setup, escalation in chapter two, a reason to continue in chapter three, at least two payoffs.

Common failures: a trusted villain who loses by stupidity; a state file that is never updated; foreshadowing planted and forgotten; a revision that quietly breaks a later chapter; a cast that grows without functions.
