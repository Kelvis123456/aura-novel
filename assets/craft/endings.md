# endings
## When to use
Use when a story builds well but ends disappointingly, when the climax feels unearned, or when the resolution does not complete the character's arc.

## Core rules
- The best endings feel both inevitable ("of course") and surprising ("I didn't see that coming"). Inevitable alone is predictable; surprising alone is arbitrary. Achieve both by planting seeds, rooting the ending in character and theme, and subverting surface expectations while fulfilling deeper ones.
- Endings are promises kept. Genre, character setup and theme make implicit promises; keep them in an unexpected way.
- Climax: answers the central dramatic question, forces the protagonist's final choice, results from story logic rather than external intervention, and requires the character to use what they have learned or become.
- Falling action: shows the immediate aftermath, begins revealing implications, lets the reader breathe. Denouement: closes the main plot, addresses major subplots, shows the new normal, ends on a resonant image or moment.
- E1 Arbitrary: resolution not seeded. Trace backward to find what the story actually points toward, plant seeds, make the climax emerge from character growth.
- E2 Predictable: subvert the how while fulfilling the what; reach the emotional destination by an unexpected route.
- E3 Unearned: no deus ex machina. The protagonist's choice must drive the resolution; establish earlier what is needed at the climax; if help arrives, the protagonist enabled it.
- E4 Expanding: endings contract. Answer the central question, introduce no major new questions late, and distinguish intentional open endings from accidental unfinished ones.
- E5 Overexplained: no character speeches stating the theme, a brief and resonant epilogue at most, some threads left implicit; end on action or image, not summary.
- E6 Pacing: rushed (add falling action), endless (end quickly after the essential resolution), anticlimactic (match the climax to the stakes). Denouement should be shorter than the buildup and proportional to story length.
- Types: Closed (risk: too neat), Open, Ambiguous (unsatisfying if arbitrary), Twist (cheap if unearned), Circular (contrived if forced).
- Arc completion: positive arc, the character proves the new truth through action; negative arc, the fall completes and feels inevitable from their choices; flat arc, the steadfast truth is vindicated and changes the world. Test: does the protagonist prove change through action, not statement?
- Subplots: the more space a subplot received, the more resolution it needs. Plot-connected must resolve; character-developing should resolve or show a new state; thematic mirrors and world texture can stay open.
- Meet genre expectations at the macro level and surprise at the micro level.
- Final image: resonate with the opening, embody theme, compress meaning. Avoid summary, logistics of next steps, excessive epilogue.
- Victory should cost something. Dark endings must come from story logic, not shock. A sequel hook may exist alongside a complete resolution.
- Eucatastrophe: a sudden turn to good must be set up, earned, feel like grace not luck, and cost something.

## Diagnose
- Does the resolution use only what was established, and could a reread reveal the seeds?
- Does the protagonist's choice, not luck or an outsider, resolve the climax?
- Is there any surprise in the how, even if the what is expected?
- Is the central dramatic question clearly answered, and did the scope narrow?
- Does anyone state the theme aloud?
- Is the falling action too short, too long, or smaller than the buildup?
- Does the protagonist prove change through action?
- Are subplots resolved in proportion to their space?
- What is the final image, and does it resonate?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give a diagnosis: name the ending problem (arbitrary, predictable, unearned, expanding, overexplained, or pacing), run the inevitable-and-surprising test, check arc completion, and list which setups are missing. Then return a rewritten ending that fixes it: make the protagonist's choice decisive, use only established elements (mark any seed that must be planted earlier, with a one-line suggestion of where), cut theme speeches, size the falling action correctly, and finish on a concrete resonant image. Keep the author's characters, events and voice.

## Deep notes
The root fix for most ending trouble is to trace backward: ask what ending the story actually points toward, then either write that ending or revise the story to point toward the one you want. An ending cannot be repaired in isolation, because it is the culmination of everything before it.

Diagnostic checklists per state:
- E1 Arbitrary: the ending uses only established elements; the climax choice emerges from the arc; the resolution matches the story's internal logic; on a reread the foreshadowing would be visible. Also ask whether this is the ending this specific story needed.
- E2 Predictable: at least one element subverts expectation; the path contains surprise; the genre promise is fulfilled unexpectedly; the emotional destination is reached by an unexpected route. Add a complication that does not change the destination but changes the journey, and consider which genre element can be inverted.
- E3 Unearned: the protagonist's choice drives resolution; skills and growth used at the climax were shown developing; no new powers or allies appear to save the day; the arc's completion is necessary for victory. Example fix for a cavalry that arrives just in time: have the protagonist's earlier act summon or enable it, or let the protagonist solve the problem with what they learned.
- E4 Expanding: loose ends must be intentional; move new questions earlier or cut them.
- E6 Pacing: rushed means the climax runs straight into the end, so add falling action and let implications register. Endless means pages continue after the tension is released, so cut to the essential and end on resonance. Anticlimactic means the resolution is smaller than the buildup, so make the climax match the stakes the story established.

Pacing numbers: a useful check is the share of the text that comes after the climax. Under about 10 percent tends to read as rushed; over about 40 percent tends to read as drawn out. Treat those as flags to inspect, not rules; a series finale can carry a longer denouement because it must resolve many threads, while a standalone thriller usually wants a short one.

Setup and payoff checks: plant a setup in the first 70 percent of the text and pay it off in the last half. If a payoff comes less than about 10 percent of the text after its setup, the setup was too thin to register and the reveal will feel sudden. List unresolved setups (guns that never fire) and payoffs with no setup (deus ex machina risk). Everything used at the climax must appear earlier, preferably more than once and in a context that does not announce its importance.

Ending types and risks:
- Closed suits standalone novels and genre fiction. Open suits literary fiction and series, at the risk of frustration. Ambiguous suits unreliable narrators and philosophical stories. Twist suits mystery and perception stories. Circular suits arc and theme emphasis.

Genre patterns to meet and then subvert in the how:
- Romance: couple together (happily ever after or happy for now); vary how they get there and what is sacrificed.
- Mystery: culprit revealed; vary the implications of the reveal and how the detective is changed.
- Thriller: threat neutralized; vary the cost of victory and what is lost.
- Horror: monster defeated or prevailing; consider a pyrrhic victory or persistent corruption.
- Fantasy: quest complete; the changed hero returns to a changed world.

Series: a book in a series resolves its own conflict, keeps the larger questions alive, gives satisfaction and anticipation, and avoids a pure cliffhanger. A finale resolves all major threads, may run a longer denouement, pays off long-term setups and ends arcs definitively. Sequel bait, an ending that exists mainly to set up the next book, denies this one a complete arc; a hook can sit beside a real resolution.

Final image: strong images resonate with the opening to show change, embody the theme visually, leave an emotional aftertaste and compress meaning into a moment. Weak ones are summary or explanation, logistics of next steps, excessive epilogue, and happy group scenes that were not earned.

Before and after for an overexplained ending: "I finally understand that family means showing up," she said. Replace with her arriving at the door of the brother she stopped calling, carrying the broken kettle he always asked her to fix, and letting the scene end when he opens the door.

The Nihilistic Swerve, a dark ending that contradicts the emotional journey, reads as shock value; derive the darkness from story logic.

Eucatastrophe, Tolkien's sudden turn to good after seeming certain disaster: it must be set up, earned by the character's growth, feel like grace and not luck, and cost something.

When asked to diagnose, also build a promise inventory: list the implicit promises made by genre, character setup and theme, decide which must be kept and which subplots need resolution in proportion to the space they received, and name what the reader must see to feel satisfied (new state, answered question, resonant image).
