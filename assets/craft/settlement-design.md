# settlement-design
## When to use
Use when creating cities, towns, villages or districts that need spatial logic and layered history, so they feel lived-in rather than built to suit the plot.

## Core rules
- Build from ten principles: geographic determinism, functional necessity, network emergence (settlements exist within systems), layered development (accretion, not comprehensive planning), power projection, resource constraint, cultural expression, adaptive reuse, disaster response, and spatial stratification.
- Choose values across six parameter categories. Site: water access, defensive position, resource proximity, trade-route placement (crossroads, harbors, passes), climate, spiritual significance.
- Morphology: street pattern (grid, radial, organic, hierarchical), density gradient, boundary definition, building typology, open space, vertical development. Function: economic base, defense systems, civic organization, social infrastructure, religious facilities, knowledge centers.
- Infrastructure: water management, transport network, food storage and distribution, energy, waste, communication. Socio-spatial: elite spaces, common quarters, marginalized zones, transitional spaces, contested territories, ethnoreligious districts. Symbolic: monumental expression, ritual pathways, collective memory sites, identity boundaries, cosmic alignment, status signification.
- Classify by primary function (market, administrative, religious, military outpost, production, agricultural, transport hub, knowledge center), morphology (planned grid, concentric, linear, organic cluster, radial, composite, dispersed, nucleated), and geographic setting (river, coastal port, hill town, valley, plains, mountain, island, desert oasis).
- Match scale: isolated homestead, hamlet (no specialization), village (simple division of labor), town (some specialization), city (complex organization), metropolis (regional dominance), megalopolis, imperial capital. Institutions must fit the population; a hamlet has no thieves' guild district.
- Use known evolution sequences, for example camp, hamlet, village, town, city; military outpost to frontier settlement to regional center to capital; shrine to pilgrimage site to temple complex to holy city; trading post to market town to commercial center; mining camp to industrial town; fishing settlement to port to naval center.
- Choose growth patterns: concentric expansion, axial development along corridors, leap-frog, infill densification, satellite formation, linear extension, cellular accretion of districts, polycentric merging.
- Apply crisis-adaptation pairs: fire leads to rebuilding with fire prevention; flood to raised construction; disease to sanitation; siege to fortification; resource depletion to diversification; overcrowding to peripheral or vertical growth; political collapse to fragmentation and repurposing.
- Define district types: commercial, elite residential, common residential, industrial, religious, administrative, entertainment, military. Name them by function, class, history, position, ethnicity or occupation.
- Work in order: site analysis, founding purpose and initial morphology, growth phases and specialization, historical layering (crises, adaptive responses, an architectural palimpsest, contested spaces), contemporary state (active and declining areas, tensions).
- Let infrastructure shape form: aqueducts create neighborhoods, markets cluster near gates, tanners locate downriver.
- Avoid Designer's Map Syndrome (add at least one disruption layer such as a fire, flood or invasion that left scars), Functional Perfection (include things that do not serve the plot), Scale Implausibility (use a real-world analog), Missing Infrastructure (settle water, waste, food and fuel), and Homogeneous Population (at least three distinct zones with transition areas where tension shows).

## Diagnose
- Why is the settlement on this exact site: water, defense, resources, trade?
- Does population size match the institutions and districts described?
- Where do water, food, fuel and waste come in and go out?
- What disaster has reshaped it, and where can its scars be seen?
- Is the layout too symmetrical or too convenient?
- Are there at least three zones with different wealth, character and age?
- Where do rich and poor, old and new, native and foreign meet?
- Does anything exist here only for its inhabitants, with no use for the protagonists?
- How did its function change over time, and which buildings were repurposed?

## How to apply
Reply in the language of the request. Return a paste-ready settlement sheet with these headings: Overview (name, function type, scale, population estimate); Site (geography and the reasons for the location); Morphology (street pattern, boundaries, density, skyline); Districts (four to eight, each with character, residents and one telling detail); Infrastructure (water, transport, food, energy, waste, communication); Social Geography (elite, common, marginal, contested, transitional zones); History (founding, growth phases, two crises with their physical marks); Symbolic Layer (monuments, ritual routes, memory sites); Current Tensions and Changes; Story Hooks. Mark invented content as proposals, match scale to a named real-world analog, and keep it consistent with the user's lore.


## Deep notes

Option lists to pick from:
- Water access: rivers, lakes, coastlines, springs, wells. Defensive position: elevation, natural barriers, visibility. Resources nearby: mines, timber, fertile soil, wildlife. Climate: temperature, rainfall, seasonal pattern. Spiritual draw: sacred sites, astronomical alignments.
- Boundary: walls, natural features, or markers only. Open space: plazas, parks, markets, fields. Building typology: name the dominant form and the material it comes from, since local materials and technology limit what can be built.
- Economic base: agricultural, industrial, commercial, administrative. Defense: fortifications, surveillance, escape routes. Civic: governance spaces. Social infrastructure: meeting places and recreation. Knowledge: schools, libraries, universities.
- Water: supply, drainage, waste disposal. Transport: roads, bridges, canals, ports. Food: granaries, markets, warehouses. Energy: fuel sources and distribution. Waste: disposal, recycling, sanitation. Communication: message systems and signals.
- Social space: elite areas (high-status residential and ceremonial), common quarters, marginalized zones for low-status or excluded people, transitional spaces between social domains, contested territories with disputed ownership, and ethnoreligious districts where a group clusters.
- Symbolic layer: monuments expressing power and identity, procession routes, markers of historical events, identity boundaries between groups, astronomical or religious orientation of streets, and rank signaled by position (higher, closer to the center, upwind).

Type descriptions to choose from. By function: a market settlement is trade-focused; an administrative center is governance-oriented; a religious complex is faith-centered; a military outpost is defense-focused; a production center does manufacturing or extraction; an agricultural community is farm-based; a transport hub exists to move people and goods; a knowledge center holds education and research. By morphology: planned grid (regular street network); concentric (rings around a core); linear (along a path or feature); organic cluster (irregular, emergent); radial (streets from a central hub); composite (several morphological sections glued together); dispersed (scattered buildings with no center); nucleated village (tightly clustered around a focal point). By setting: river settlement, coastal port, hill town, valley community, plains settlement, mountain outpost, island, desert oasis.

Growth patterns, described: concentric expansion adds rings; axial development follows transport corridors; leap-frog growth leaves gaps; infill densification fills empty space inside; satellite formation spawns secondary settlements around a primary; linear extension grows on one axis; cellular accretion adds whole districts; polycentric evolution lets several growing centers merge.

Setting adaptations:
- Fantasy: a city built around an arcane energy source; architecture for several species with different needs; mystical protection that shapes the urban form; a layout reflecting divine will; portals or teleportation that change where markets and gates sit.
- Science fiction: enclosed dome cities on hostile worlds; orbital habitats with artificial gravity limits; underground complexes; multi-species cohabitation; zero-gravity layout that ignores floors and ceilings.
- Post-apocalyptic: reuse of pre-collapse structures; layouts built around scarcity and defense; sites chosen to avoid contamination; building styles that follow lost capability; communities clustered around surviving infrastructure.

District naming. By function: Market Quarter, Warehouse District. By class: Noble Quarter, Craftsmen's Row. By history: Old Town, New District. By position: North Ward, Riverside. By ethnicity or culture: Foreign District, a species quarter. By occupation: Tanner's Lane, Merchant Row. Typical character: commercial districts hold markets, shops, warehouses and trade halls; elite residential has large homes, gardens and quiet streets; common residential is dense with narrow streets; industrial means workshops, manufacturing and pollution; religious means temples, monasteries and sacred space; administrative holds government buildings, courts and archives; entertainment holds theaters, taverns and pleasure houses; military holds barracks, armories and training grounds.

Worked example. Take a river-ford market town of about 2,500 people. The ford explains the site; the first market lay at the crossing and became the square. The old palisade burned in a fire, so the center was rebuilt in stone with a narrow lane left as a firebreak (the disruption layer). Tanners and dyers sit downstream and downwind of the square, the cheapest housing is nearest them, and the prosperous merchants' street runs uphill on the sunny side. A shrine at a spring on the far bank gives a procession route that crosses the ford once a year, and that crossing is where the town's old and new families meet. At 2,500 it has a few full-time specialists and a weekly market, but no thieves' guild quarter and no university.

Step prompts per phase:
- Site: key geographic features, resource availability, defensive potential, connectivity, climate.
- Foundation: why it was founded, the initial morphology, the core structures, the original boundary.
- Growth: expansion pattern, district specialization, infrastructure networks, zones of social stratification.
- Historical layering: crises, how people adapted, buildings from different eras stacked on one another, spaces whose ownership is disputed.

Extra failure signs: perfect symmetry with no historical disruption; every element serving the current plot; scale mismatch between population and institutions. Quick scale test: if characters cross the whole place in a quarter of an hour on foot, it is a town, whatever the text calls it; if a district holds a specialty trade that needs a large customer base, the population has to be large enough to feed it.
