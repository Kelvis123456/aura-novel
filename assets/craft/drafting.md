# drafting
## When to use
Use when the outline exists but the draft is not happening, when the page stays blank or progress stalls, or when a writer keeps editing instead of moving forward.

## Core rules
- Drafting is discovery, not transcription. The first draft exists to be revised; its only job is to exist. A finished bad draft beats an unfinished good one.
- Drafting is generative, forward and quantity-focused with the internal editor off. Editing is evaluative, backward and quality-focused. Do not edit while drafting: move forward, fix it later.
- D1, Can't Start: causes are perfectionism, anxiety, over-preparation, a vague outline. Lower the bar ("100 bad words"), write a zero-draft scene, start with dialogue only, or write "What happens in this scene is..." and describe it.
- D2, Start But Can't Continue: causes are editing while drafting or stopping at a dead end. Skip to a scene you can write, use placeholders such as [SOMETHING HAPPENS HERE], stop mid-sentence so resuming is easy, and do not re-read earlier work before writing.
- D3, Middle Stall: the outline is incomplete, a structural problem has surfaced, or direction is unclear. Write toward the ending, write out of order, or ask "What's the worst thing that could happen now?"
- D4, Can't Finish (70-90% done): fear of judgment or an inadequate ending. Write the ending now and fill gaps later; the ending can be revised.
- D5, Taking Too Long: constant revision disguised as drafting. Raise daily goals (500, then 1000, then 2000 words), time-box sessions, choose speed over quality for the rest of the draft.
- D6, Draft Reveals a Story Problem: distinguish prose problems from structure problems. If prose, push through. If structure, take one day to re-outline, then continue. If character, write the scene wrong, mark it and keep going. Options: push through, re-outline, follow the draft, or write both versions.
- Silence the editor: set a timer and do not stop, write in a junk file, change tool or location, dictate, or write with the screen off.
- Methods: Daily Quota (500 words/day is sustainable, 1,000 moderate, 2,000 intensive); Session Method (25 minutes on, 5 off; 60-minute power hour; 15-30 minute sprints); Scene Method (one scene per session); Out-of-Order Method (write what excites you, assemble later); Free Writing (5-20 minutes, hand never stops, correct nothing, write "I don't know what to write" if stuck).
- When a scene will not work: write it badly on purpose, write only the dialogue, summarize it in brackets, skip it, or ask whether it needs to exist.
- Hating the draft is normal; keep going and do not re-read until it is done.
- Do not compare a first draft with published novels, which had many drafts.

## Diagnose
- Is the writer not starting, stalling early, stuck in the middle, unable to finish, too slow, or facing a story problem?
- Is the writer editing while drafting or re-reading before writing?
- Is the outline too vague to write from?
- Is the bar for a first draft too high?
- Is there a daily goal or session system?
- Do they know the ending?
- Is the problem prose quality or story structure?
- Is the current scene unclear, or is it a scene that should not exist?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give a short diagnosis: name the drafting state (D1-D6), the likely cause, and the one intervention that fits, in plain, encouraging terms and a few lines. Then produce a rough, forward-moving continuation or zero draft of the scene from what the user supplied: dialogue-led if the scene is stuck, bracketed placeholders for anything undecided, no polish, no backtracking edits to what already exists. Follow with one concrete small target for the next session (for example a word count or the next scene to write). Do not push the user to revise or polish until the draft is complete.

## Deep notes
Prerequisites: a draft session works best with an outline or at least a clear sense of the story, characters defined enough to write, and a setting clear enough to describe. If those are missing, the block is a planning problem, not a drafting problem.

Under stress the brain shifts from the creative cortex toward the survival system, so anxiety about quality impairs generation; the fixes are physical and procedural rather than motivational.

Reading the states more precisely:
- D1 key questions: Is the outline too vague to write from? Is the bar too high? Is there fear of the blank page? Are you waiting to feel ready? Add: set a start date and keep it regardless of readiness. The outline is done when the deadline arrives.
- D2 checklist: not re-reading previous work before writing, moving forward rather than polishing backward, stopping mid-scene rather than at scene ends, and using placeholders for stuck parts. Also drop word-count goals sharply for a few sessions, and disable backspace or turn the screen off.
- D3 key questions: Is the story working? Is the draft revealing a structural problem? Is motivation flagging because direction is unclear? Do you know the ending? Causes include an incomplete middle outline, a story problem found mid-draft, the long slog between an exciting start and end, and a lost sense of why the story matters. If the cause is boredom, jump to an exciting scene; if structural, do a brief outline review then continue; if momentum, shrink daily goals and rebuild the habit.
- D4: writers avoid finishing because a finished draft can be judged, because the next step is uncertain, because the ending feels inadequate, or because they are attached to the process. Set a deadline and tell someone, commit to a word count regardless of quality, and write the ending now.
- D5 checklist: a daily goal exists and is met; nothing beyond the last paragraph is re-read before starting; sentences are not revised after writing; the work is drafting, not perpetual revision. Track word count publicly, time-box sessions at an hour regardless of output, and adopt the dirty-draft stance for the rest of the book.
- D6 options in full: push through and fix in revision (fastest); pause and re-outline (significant, for a fundamentally broken foundation); follow the draft and let the story go where it wants (discovery); write both versions and compare later (exploratory). Ask what specifically feels wrong before choosing.

Pace arithmetic: 500 words a day is about 180,000 words a year, 1,000 a day about 365,000, 2,000 a day about 730,000. A 90,000-word novel is roughly 180 days at 500 words a day, 90 at 1,000 and 45 at 2,000. Pick the lowest number you will hit on a bad day.

Session setups:
- Scene method: one scene per session, a discrete goal that can be finished.
- Out-of-order: it keeps enthusiasm and bypasses stuck points.
- Free writing, in five steps: set 5 to 20 minutes; keep the hand moving; correct nothing; if stuck, write "I don't know what to write"; at the end, mark passages worth keeping.

Complete-stuck routine, in the order to try: change location, change tool, lower the bar to 100 words, skip ahead to a scene you can write, write about being stuck, explain the scene aloud and transcribe it, set a timer for ten minutes of forced writing.

Scene that will not work: write it badly on purpose, write only the dialogue, summarize it in brackets such as [Character confronts the villain about the letter], skip it with a placeholder, or ask whether the scene needs to exist at all.

Environment: a consistent location acts as a habit trigger. Use a distraction-free app, block the internet, silence notifications and put the phone in another room. Consistent time of day matters more than which time; mornings often work.

Worked example. A writer has a finished outline and has avoided chapter one for three weeks. Diagnosis: D1, with perfectionism as the cause. Plan: tonight, write "What happens in this scene is..." and describe the scene in plain sentences for ten minutes; tomorrow, turn those sentences into dialogue only, with [DESCRIBE KITCHEN] where setting would go; the day after, set a target of 500 bad words and stop mid-sentence. Do not re-read before the third session.

Anti-pattern list with the fix for each:
- The Endless Outliner plans forever; set a start date.
- The Sentence Polisher revises each sentence before the next; turn the screen off and go forward only.
- The Perfect First-Drafter expects near-final prose; remember that professional first drafts are rough and compare first drafts only with first drafts.
- The Mood Writer waits for inspiration; professionals write on schedule and inspiration follows action.
- The Middle Abandoner starts many drafts and finishes none; finish one bad draft before starting another.

Save prose polish and all revision until the whole draft exists.
