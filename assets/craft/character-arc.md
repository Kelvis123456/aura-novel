# character-arc
## When to use
Use when a character feels static, a transformation feels unearned or abrupt, or the character serves the plot without an inner journey. It helps define the false belief at the core of the arc and map how it breaks.

## Core rules
- Treat the arc as the inner journey: the transformation from one sort of person to a different sort under pressure. The plot is what happens; the arc is who the character becomes. External plot supplies the pressure.
- Positive change arc components, in order: Lie (false belief about self or world, formed by a backstory wound, the "ghost"), Want (what they think they need, driven by the lie), Need (what would actually fulfill them, invisible at the start), Catalyst (story forces confrontation with the lie), Struggle (they resist), Truth (they accept reality and drop the lie), New Self (they act from the transformed perspective).
- Negative change arc (tragedy): Potential, Flaw, Temptation (the easy path that feeds the flaw), Descent (choices compound the flaw), Point of No Return (redemption offered and rejected), Consequence (the flaw destroys what the character valued).
- Flat arc: the character already holds the truth. Truth, World in Conflict, Testing (truth is pressured but holds), Influence (steadfastness changes others), Vindication. The character changes the world rather than being changed.
- Make want and need different. If the character wants exactly what they need, there is no tension; create a gap between the external goal and the internal need.
- Name the lie explicitly and trace it to a backstory wound. If the lie cannot be stated in one sentence, the arc has no foundation.
- Story events must directly challenge the lie. A change the events did not force is unearned.
- Real change is resisted. Write scenes where the character doubles down on the lie before letting go of it. A sudden flip without struggle reads as abrupt.
- Map arc to structure: Setup (lie established, want activated); First Plot Point (commits while still believing the lie); Rising Action (want pursued, lie reinforced); Midpoint (mirror moment, a glimpse of truth); Complications (lie vs. truth); Dark Night (lie fully fails, crisis); Climax (truth embraced, or rejected in tragedy); Resolution (new self demonstrated).
- Show change through external choices and actions, never by telling the reader the character changed (the Informed Arc).
- A mentor points a direction; the character must walk the path. Change caused only by being told the truth is the Magic Mentor failure.
- Trauma creates conditions; the arc is what the character does with it. Terrible events alone are not transformation.
- Even admirable protagonists need a blind spot. No flaw, no lie, no arc.
- Plant seeds early so the epiphany feels inevitable in retrospect; avoid the Instant Epiphany.
- Not every character needs a full arc; do not add transformation where it does not serve the story.
- Example lies: "I'm not worthy of love", "Power is the only protection", "Trust leads to betrayal".

## Diagnose
- Is the character meaningfully different at the end than at the start?
- What exactly does the character believe at the start that is false?
- Which backstory wound produced that belief?
- Does the character want something different from what they need?
- Did the story's events force this change, or does the author simply announce it?
- Where does the character resist, and do they ever double down on the lie?
- Is there a midpoint glimpse of truth and a dark night where the lie fully fails?
- Does the final act show the new self through a costly choice?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give a short diagnosis: the arc type you detect (positive, negative, flat), the lie, want and need as far as the passage shows them, which beat the passage sits on, and which anti-pattern or gap you see (no transformation, unearned, abrupt, unclear lie, missing struggle, informed arc, instant epiphany). If the lie is not inferable, state your assumption inline instead of inventing silently, and do not impose an arc type the author did not choose. Then return a rewritten passage that fixes the main problem: replace announced change with a visible choice, add a resistance beat, or tie the event to the lie. Keep the author's voice, names, setting and plot facts. End by naming what you changed and why.


## Deep notes

### Six-step working method
- Ask about the lie: what false belief does the character hold? Do not choose it for the writer.
- Find the ghost: which backstory wound created the lie?
- Separate want from need: what they pursue versus what they actually require.
- Map the transformation: place the key beats on the story's structure.
- Check for resistance: where does the character fight the change?
- Verify the ending: is the new self shown through action rather than narration?
- Do not prescribe an arc type, do not add transformation where it does not serve the story, and do not insist that every character carry a full arc.

### More lies to draw from
- "My value comes from achievement." "The world is fundamentally hostile." "I'm not capable." "My wound defines me." "I don't need anyone." Also: "Asking for help is failure", "If I'm perfect, I'll be loved", "Other people always leave", "I must control everything", "Emotions are dangerous".
- Ghosts that produce them: parental abandonment, public humiliation, betrayal by someone trusted, failure at a crucial moment, survivor's guilt, a broken promise with consequences, being used by someone they loved.

### Lie to behavior to scene
- State the lie as a sentence, then list three behaviors it causes in ordinary life (not plot). Each behavior becomes a scene in Setup.
- Then write the opposite truth as one sentence ("Asking for help is how work gets finished"). The climax choice is the action that embodies that sentence at a cost.
- Want and need example: want is to win the promotion; need is to accept that worth is not earned. If winning the promotion would also satisfy the need, the gap is too small, so make winning require the old behavior and losing require the new one.

### Beat-by-beat pattern for a positive arc
- Setup: show the lie operating in a small, concrete choice. The reader should be able to predict the character's reaction to a request for help.
- First Plot Point: the character commits to the want using the lie as the method.
- Rising Action: the method works at first, which is what reinforces the lie.
- Midpoint: a mirror moment. A glimpse of the truth, often through someone else's behavior, which the character explains away.
- Complications: the lie starts costing more than it earns; allies suffer or leave.
- Dark Night: the lie fully fails. The character loses the want and sees no way forward.
- Climax: the character must choose between the lie's safe option and the truth's costly one. Show the choice, not a thought about it.
- Resolution: a small scene that repeats the Setup situation with a different response.

### Before and after patterns
- Informed arc. Before: "She had learned to trust people." After: the same character, in the scene that earlier made her check the locked drawer, leaves the drawer open and goes out with the key left on the table.
- Instant epiphany. Before: a speech from a friend and then "she understood." After: plant two earlier moments where she nearly does the right thing and pulls back; the epiphany then completes a movement already begun.
- Magic mentor. Before: the mentor states the lesson. After: the mentor asks one question or withholds help, and the character does the work of reaching the conclusion, possibly in the mentor's absence.
- Trauma equals transformation. Before: backstory dump of loss, then the character is "different". After: show what they do with the loss, the avoidance habits it produced and the moment they act against them.
- Abrupt change. Insert at least two doubling-down scenes: the character meets evidence against the lie and responds with more of the lie (more control, more distance), and the cost rises each time.

### Negative arc specifics
- Temptation must be genuinely attractive, not obviously evil. The reader should feel the pull.
- Descent works through compounding: each choice makes the next harder to refuse.
- The Point of No Return needs a visible offer of redemption (a person, a chance, a confession available) that is rejected. Without the offer, it is merely bad luck.
- Consequence should destroy the specific thing the character valued, not something generic.

### Flat arc specifics
- The flat protagonist changes others. Show at least one secondary character shifting because the protagonist did not.
- Testing means real pressure: the truth costs them something and they hold anyway. A flat hero who is never tempted is a Perfect Protagonist.

### Diagnosing a hollow win
- When the protagonist defeats the villain but it feels empty, ask what is different about them at the end. If nothing, they solved a problem without an arc.
- Probe what they believed at the start that was false, then what would have happened had they learned nothing. The victory must require becoming someone new; otherwise it is problem-solving.
- Ask what they would have to stop believing in order to win authentically.
