# worldbuilding
## When to use
Use when a setting feels thin: a painted backdrop, institutions that seem designed last week, an economy that makes no sense, cultures or non-human species that are shallow. It diagnoses which world-level problem is present and prescribes a specific fix.

## Core rules
- Treat the central principle as law: worlds fail when they feel designed rather than evolved. A convincing world has history, internal logic, and processes that run independently of the plot.
- Classify the problem into one world state. W1 Backdrop World: setting has no independent logic. W2 World Without Consequences: technology or magic exists but has not transformed society. W3 Institutions Without History: organizations feel new. W4 Economy Doesn't Make Sense: trade without supply chains, arbitrary prices. W5 Shallow Belief Systems: religion as flavor with no theological depth. W6 Culture Without Depth: traditions feel random, surface aesthetic only. W7 Flat Non-Humans: humans in costume. W7.5 Generic Language: names sound like English, no linguistic texture.
- Apply the Consequence Cascade to every major speculative element. First order: direct practical effects (who gains immediate advantage, what becomes obsolete, what the technical limits are). Second order: systemic adaptations (how economic structures adapt, how power structures respond, what resistance arises). Third order: cultural evolution (what new language emerges, what ethical questions arise, what becomes normalized).
- Ask of any technology or magic: what is the initial divergence from our world, who gains power, what becomes obsolete, how would the powerful try to control it.
- Ask of any institution: when was it founded, what crises has it survived, what are its internal contradictions.
- Ask of any economy: what is the fundamental scarcity, how is value determined, what is the underground economy.
- Ask of any belief system: what explains existence, how do beliefs affect daily decisions, what are the schisms and debates.
- Fix the Monoculture (one unified culture for a whole planet or species) with regional variation, class differences and schisms.
- Fix the Convenient Technology (it exists when the plot needs it) by tracing its consequence cascade.
- Fix the Static History (nothing changes for centuries) with recent disruptions and reforms in progress.
- Fix the Evil Empire (uniformly evil antagonist nation) with internal debates and ordinary people.
- Fix the Rubber Forehead Alien (cosmetic differences only) by starting from biology, tracing to cognition, then to culture.
- Go deep when an element is central to plot, will be examined closely, or creates ongoing conflict.
- Stay shallow when an element is background detail, the POV character would not know the depth, or mystery is more interesting than explanation.
- Route each diagnosis to the right deeper tool: consequences, economy, belief, governance, language, or settlement design.

## Diagnose
- Does anything in this world happen, change or decay when no character is looking?
- Which speculative element has not yet changed who holds power, what people eat, trade, fear or say?
- Which institution has no founding date, no survived crisis and no internal contradiction?
- Can I name the scarcity, the currency, and the black market?
- Does any belief change what a character does on an ordinary Tuesday?
- Is any culture or species a single trait repeated across every member?
- Is the history frozen, with no recent disruption or reform underway?
- Is the antagonist society given reasons its own people support it?
- Would the non-human species still make sense if its body were swapped for a human one?
- Where am I going deep out of habit when the story only needs a glimpse?

## How to apply
Reply in the language of the request. First name the world state (W1 to W7.5) that best fits the draft and quote the symptom you saw in the user's text. Then return a paste-ready proposal with these headings: Diagnosis (one paragraph), Consequence Cascade (first, second and third order for the main speculative element, three to five bullets each, with concrete invented specifics), Institutions (for each: founding, survived crisis, internal contradiction), Economy and Belief (scarcity, value, underground economy; what the belief explains, daily effect, one schism), Anti-patterns Found (each with its fix applied to this world), and Depth Decisions (which elements to develop deeply, which to leave shallow or mysterious, and why). Keep invented names consistent with the user's existing lore, mark every new invention as a proposal, and end with the two or three highest-leverage fixes in priority order.

## Deep notes

How each world state shows up, and what a fix looks like in practice.
- W1, backdrop world: the setting only exists when a character looks at it. Give it one process that runs without the plot, such as a failed harvest, a guild election or a raised tax, then let the plot collide with it.
- W2, world without consequences: magic or technology exists, yet markets, laws, weddings, wars and food match ours. Run the cascade on that single element and write at least one change per order. Example: a spell that preserves food does not just better supply soldiers; it ends seasonal famine, shifts power away from granary owners, makes winter wars normal, and eventually gives people a word for the dread of the spell failing.
- W3, institutions without history: an institution is an answer to an old problem. Give it a founding date, the crisis that shaped it, a rule that survives from that crisis though nobody remembers why, and an internal contradiction such as a vow of poverty beside a vast endowment. A reform in progress proves it is alive.
- W4, economy that does not make sense: name the fundamental scarcity first, then how value is measured, who moves goods, and what the underground economy sells. Prices follow distance, risk and scarcity, not the plot's convenience.
- W5, shallow belief: ask what the belief explains, what it demands on an ordinary day (what to eat, whom to marry, how to bury the dead), and where it has split. One schism with two named positions, each with a sensible argument, does more than a long pantheon list.
- W6, culture without depth: traditions feel random when they have no cause. Trace each custom to a past pressure (a famine, a migration, a defeat) and let a younger generation question it.
- W7, flat non-humans: begin with the body, then the senses, then the cognition those allow, then the social forms that cognition makes possible. A species that cannot hear needs no word for "listen" and may have no sense of privacy in speech.
- W7.5, generic language: give a culture a small recurring set of sounds, a naming convention, a few untranslated words for things its people care about, and idioms drawn from its own economy and geography.

Writing a cascade. Write the first-order effect, then ask "and then what?" twice. A good chain reads: element, who gains, who loses, which institution responds, what the powerful do to control it, what resistance forms, what becomes normal, what word or ethic appears. If a step is a general statement ("society changes"), replace it with an event, a person or an object.

Control question. The powerful try to capture any new capability. Ask who licenses it, who taxes it, who bans it, who smuggles it and who is ruined by it. The underground economy is the quickest proof that a rule has teeth.

Anti-pattern repairs with sample moves.
- Monoculture: split a people into at least three groups by region, class and creed, each with a grievance against another.
- Convenient technology: list the tool's inputs, maintenance and limits, then check whether the plot ever pays those costs.
- Static history: add a disruption within the last twenty years (a plague, a succession dispute, a new road) that people still argue about.
- Evil empire: give the antagonist society a faction that wants peace, a decent bureaucrat, and a reason ordinary people feel safe under it.
- Rubber forehead alien: change the biology first, then check that customs, laws and humor changed too.

Depth decisions. Mark each element deep, shallow or mysterious before drafting.
- Deep when it drives the plot, will be examined by a viewpoint character, or causes recurring conflict.
- Shallow when it is texture only. One precise detail beats a paragraph.
- Mysterious when the viewpoint character cannot know the full picture, or when the open question is more engaging than the answer. Keep the hidden answer consistent in your own notes.
- A common failure is going deep out of habit. If a reader will never meet an element twice, a glimpse is enough.

Edge cases.
- A world can be convincing with very few worked-out systems if the ones shown behave consistently.
- If a cascade makes the setting grim or comic by accident, decide whether to follow it or to cap it with a stated limit in the world's rules.
- Keep invented specifics consistent with lore already on the page, and treat new ones as proposals until the author accepts them.
