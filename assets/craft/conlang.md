# conlang
## When to use
Use when names, dialogue or alien speech sound random and need a consistent sound system, so every word feels like it comes from one language. Works for naming languages without requiring linguistics knowledge.

## Core rules
- Hold to the central principle: languages fail when names do not sound like they belong together. Perceived coherence comes from a shared sound palette and syllable shapes, even without defined grammar.
- Match complexity to need. Flavor: 10-15 consonants, 3-5 vowels, for quick names and background cultures; output a sound palette, syllable patterns, 10-20 sample names. Naming: 15-22 consonants, 5-7 vowels, for main characters and places; output a full inventory, syllable templates, 50+ words. Full: 20-35 consonants, 7-12 vowels, for closely examined languages; adds phonotactic constraints and enables morphology.
- Define syllable templates first, then regenerate any name that does not fit. Feel guide: flowing (CV, CVV: ta-ri-a), balanced (CV, CVC: kor-tan), complex (CCVC, CVCC: strak, kelth), minimal (CV only: ka-ra-na).
- Choose phonemes by frequency. Universal consonants: p, t, k, m, n, s, l, r, w, j; vowels a, i, u. Common additions: b, d, g, f, sh, h, ng, glottal stop, ch; vowels e, o. Flavor sounds: v, z, x, ny, ts; vowels open e, open o, schwa. Rare, distinctive: th, dh, q, lateral fricative; vowels ae, back unrounded u, oe, front rounded u.
- Document which phonemes are in the language and which are not. Keep the seed or the inventory so results can be regenerated consistently.
- Pick one or two distinctive features only; most natural languages are plain in most ways. Avoid the Kitchen Sink (clicks plus tones plus ejectives plus vowel harmony).
- Keep names pronounceable: simpler syllables, manageable clusters, vowels present. Avoid the Unpronounceable, such as Xq'tkhl.
- Use apostrophes only with a defined meaning (glottal stop or syllable break) and sparingly. Avoid the Apostrophe Catastrophe, such as K'tar'nak'vul.
- Avoid the Inconsistent Phonotactics ("Kthor" beside "Alina") and the Relexification (English with swapped words; identify what should be grammaticalized differently and which concepts have no English equivalent).
- Avoid the Human Alien: start from biology, derive the vocal apparatus, then the possible sounds and the concepts the species' cognition marks or cannot translate.
- Add depth with registers (formal and informal), dialects, and historical layers (archaic or liturgical forms, loanwords, language death).
- Reflect the world in the language: economy gives trade and value vocabulary; power gives registers, honorifics and forbidden words; belief gives sacred language, taboo and liturgy; geography gives dialects, contact languages and trade pidgins; history gives archaic layers and borrowings.
- Do not develop full grammar unless asked, do not demand authenticity over usability, and let the writer decide what works.
- Diagnose by state: L1 no language (generic names, mixed cultures); L2 relexified English; L3 inconsistent phonology; L4 missing depth (no registers, dialects, history); L5 biology mismatch.

## Diagnose
- Do all names in this culture share recurring sounds and syllable shapes?
- Can I list which sounds are present and which are deliberately absent?
- Does any name break the syllable template?
- Can a reader sound it out on first sight?
- Is this just English with different words, with no concept that English lacks?
- Am I using more than two exotic features at once?
- Are apostrophes or diacritics decorative or meaningful?
- Does a non-human speaker have the body that would produce these sounds?
- Do power, religion, trade and history leave marks on vocabulary and register?

## How to apply
Reply in the language of the request, keeping the conlang words themselves in Latin script. First ask or infer what the language is for (names only, dialogue, full grammar), the desired feel, any sounds to include or exclude, and existing names that must fit. Then return a paste-ready language sheet with these headings: Complexity Level; Sound Palette (consonants, vowels, rare sounds excluded); Syllable Templates (with feel); Orthography Notes (any apostrophe or digraph rule); Sample Words (10-20 for flavor, 50 or more for naming, grouped as personal names, place names, short words, long words); Cultural Layer (register or honorific hints, sacred or taboo terms, loanword tendencies); Consistency Notes (existing names rated as fitting or needing adjustment, with respelled fixes). For non-human speakers add a short biology-to-sound derivation and what would be untranslatable. Offer a reproducible reference, such as a short rule set the author can reuse.


## Deep notes

Frequency tiers for picking sounds. Universal (found in nearly all languages): consonants p, t, k, m, n, s, l, r, w, j; vowels a, i, u. Common (roughly half to most languages): b, d, g, f, sh, h, ng, glottal stop, ch; vowels e, o. Less common (a fifth to a half): v, z, x (as in Bach), ny, ts, tap r; vowels open e, open o, schwa, short i, short u. Rare (under a fifth): th (thin), dh (this), q, uvular g, lateral fricative; vowels ae (as in cat), back unrounded u, oe, front rounded u. Voiced stops b d g are more expected than the rare ones, and a language with b usually also has p.

Starter inventories you can use directly:
- Flavor, plain: p t k m n s l r w y h; a e i o u. Twelve consonants, five vowels.
- Flavor, open and musical: l r n m s t d w h; a e i o u plus the schwa; add the diphthongs ai, ei, au.
- Naming, balanced: p b t d k g m n ng f s sh h l r w y ch; a e i o u plus open e and schwa. Eighteen consonants, seven vowels. Optionally make stops aspirated for a crisp feel.
- Naming, harsh: k g glottal stop kh gh q r ng m n t; a o u e only. Avoid l, w and y entirely so the sound stays hard; allow CVC, CVCC and CCVC.
- Polynesian-like: p k m n w h plus glottal stop; a e i o u with long vowels. Open syllables only, no clusters, almost no fricatives.
- Japanese-like: k s t n h m y r w; a i u e o. Syllables are CV, V, plus a final n; r serves where English would use l.

Syllable systems by complexity. Simple: CV, CVC, V, VC with at most one consonant before and after the vowel (Japanese, Hawaiian, Swahili feel). Moderate: add CCV, CCVC, CVCC and VCC, up to two consonants at either edge (Spanish, Italian, Greek feel). Complex: up to three consonants at the start and three at the end, e.g. CCCVC and CVCCC (Russian, Polish, Georgian, German feel). Presets: Hawaiian-like allows only CV and V; Latin-like allows CV, CVC, CCV with onsets pr tr kr br dr gr pl kl bl gl sp st sk; Arabic-like allows CV, CVC, CVVC, CVCC with one consonant before the vowel; English-like allows heavy clusters like str, spr, skr; elvish-like uses CV, CVC, CVV, V, VC with preferred l r n m s t d w and diphthongs; harsh-fantasy uses CVC, CVCC, CCVC, CVCCC with k g glottal stop x q r ng and drops l, w, y.

Cluster rules to keep clusters pronounceable. Rank sounds by sonority: stops lowest, then fricatives, then nasals, then l and r, then w and y, then vowels. Onsets should rise toward the vowel (str, pl, kr); codas should fall away from it (rk, lt, nts). Safe onsets: pr pl tr tw kr kl kw br bl dr gr gl fr fl sp st sk sm sn sl sw. Safe codas: pt kt st sk nt nd mp ngk lp lt lk rp rt rk ps ts ks. Avoid dl, tl, sr, shn, pw, bw, fw.

Word shapes and stress. Short words (CV, CVC, CVCC) suit particles, pronouns and common words. Two-syllable words (CVCV, CVCVC) are the most common length. Three syllables (CVCVCV) suit nouns and verbs. Four or more (CVCVCVCV) suit compounds and formal speech. Stress: initial gives a steady rhythm; penultimate sounds natural and balanced; final gives forward momentum; weight-sensitive stress depends on syllable shape.

Family flavors to borrow. Romance-like: paired voiced and voiceless stops, five vowels, no h, no initial ng. Slavic-like: palatalization, clusters, ch, sh, zh, ts. Germanic-like: h, clusters, f v th z. Sino-Tibetan-like: tones, aspirated stops, few codas. Optional exotic features, one at most: ejectives (Caucasian, Mayan feel), implosives, clicks (very complex), tones, aspiration.

Word-building method:
1. Fix the inventory and the template. 2. Decide word length by role: names two or three syllables, places two to four, grammar words one. 3. Fill slots from the inventory, reusing a few favorite consonants so sounds recur. 4. Reject anything that breaks a cluster rule or the template. 5. Fix stress and spell consistently. 6. Record the rules so further words match.

Sample vocabulary in an open, musical language with template CV, CVC and CVV: tari (river), selo (hill), mira (star), nolu (house), kai (sea), vena (road), lorin (name of a sage), alea (dawn), sunel (oath), dari (friend), more (stone), ilu (night). Place names: Karelo, Miravan, Solune. Personal names: Talia, Orien, Vesara, Delun. In a harsh language with template CVC, CVCC and CCVC: krag (fist), dor (grave), urkh (wolf), vask (blade), gor (stone), thrak (warband), moruk (elder), zugh (fire). Names: Grakh, Dorvuk, Ushkar.
