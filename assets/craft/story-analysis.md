# story-analysis
## When to use
Use after a short story or novel chapter is drafted, to evaluate its strengths, weaknesses and improvement opportunities against its own goals.

## Core rules
- Evaluate against what the story is trying to be, not against bestsellers. Identify genre expectations first; the same trait is a flaw in one genre and a feature in another.
- Diagnose structure before prose. Do not fixate on sentence-level issues while the macro structure fails.
- Prioritize by impact: report the 3-5 highest-impact issues, note what works, and make guidance actionable. A checklist surfaces possibilities, not mandates; a missing shattering moment is critical, a slight POV wobble may be a choice.
- Short story, Narrative Foundation: one clear premise or conflict statable in one sentence, scope fitting the length, premise integrated with character stakes, implications explored.
- Short story, Character: one or two main characters with clear stakes, characterization through action and choice not description, an arc that intersects the premise, supporting characters that each serve a purpose.
- Short story, Environment: details woven into action, each element serving multiple purposes (setting, plot, theme), clear and consistent rules, environment that affects choices. Flag info-dumps and details that never matter.
- Shattering Moment: the pivotal, irreversible point that transforms everything, through personal revelation, external event, relationship transformation, internal realization or cultural collision. In science fiction, technology is, enables or prevents recovery from it, or the moment changes the character's relationship with technology.
- Short story, Scene Structure: every scene advances character and plot, the opening hook establishes character, situation and conflict, the conflict is focused, the resolution satisfies while resonating beyond.
- Short story, Technical Execution: economy of detail, consistent and purposeful POV with minimal shifts, consistent tone, purposeful dialogue with subtext, style serving the story.
- Short story, Emotional Architecture: a clear emotional throughline, strategic build and release, earned payoff, something unspoken but felt.
- Short story, Thematic Resolution: the arc completes, premise implications are addressed, the immediate conflict resolves, larger questions linger.
- Chapter, role: define the chapter's job in the novel and its plot, character and theme contribution. Momentum: the opening connects to the previous chapter, the ending creates forward pull, questions answered versus raised stay balanced. Flag chapters that end at a natural stopping point or answer everything.
- Chapter, threading: track main plot and each subplot as advanced, set up or resolved. Check character continuity, POV and voice consistency, information flow and reader-versus-character knowledge, pacing relative to neighboring chapters, and connections to the previous chapter, next chapter and wider novel.

## Diagnose
- Can the core conflict be stated in one sentence?
- Is the scope right for the length?
- Is character shown through action, and does the protagonist change?
- Is there a clear, irreversible turning point?
- Does every scene advance character and plot?
- Is POV and tone consistent?
- Is the emotional payoff earned?
- Does the resolution satisfy while still resonating?
- For a chapter: what is its job, and does the ending pull the reader on?
- Which problems are structural, and which are only preference?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give the analysis: state the genre and the story's apparent goal, list what works, then the 3-5 highest-impact weaknesses in priority order, each tied to a specific place in the text and labeled structural, character, emotional or technical. Use the short story criteria for a complete piece and the chapter criteria for a section of a longer work. Do not list every checklist miss. Then give a rewritten passage for the single most important problem (for example sharpening the opening hook, the shattering moment, or the chapter ending). Keep the author's voice, events and intent, and explain in one line what the rewrite changes.


## Deep notes

### Two modes, eight lenses each
- Short story lenses, in order: Narrative Foundation, Character Construction, Story Environment, Shattering Moment, Scene Structure, Technical Execution, Emotional Architecture, Thematic Resolution. Run them in that order; the early ones are structural and the later ones polish.
- Chapter lenses: Position in Narrative Arc, Narrative Momentum, Plot Threading, Character Continuity, POV and Voice, Information Flow, Pacing Elements, Connection Tracking.

### Red flags to scan for
- Character: described but never demonstrated; supporting characters who affect nothing; stakes unclear or abstract. Test: delete the character and see whether any scene changes.
- Environment: info-dump paragraphs; details that never matter again; a setting that does not influence events. Test: move the story to another location and see what breaks. If nothing, the setting is wallpaper.
- Chapter: ends at a natural stopping point (too easy to put down); opening ignores the previous chapter's energy; every question answered, no forward pull.

### Shattering Moment, applied
- Four checks: is it a clear turning point, is it tied to the core conflict, is the character changed irreversibly, does everything after it feel different. If the last answer is no, the moment is an event, not a shattering one.
- A quiet story can shatter on a sentence (a confession, a refusal, a word finally said). Do not demand spectacle; demand irreversibility.
- Science fiction variants to test: the technology is the moment; the technology enables it; the technology prevents recovery afterward; the moment changes the character's relationship to the technology.

### Technical execution sub-checks
- Economy of detail: every element does double duty; no background that the plot never touches; exposition rides inside action.
- POV: choice is purposeful, optimal for revealing both character and premise, shifts are minimal, narrative distance is controlled.
- Language: tone consistent, dialogue purposeful with subtext, one recognizable narrative voice.
- Emotional architecture: separate tension management (building and release, pacing of reveals, balance of explicit and implicit) from reader engagement (clear emotional stakes, strategic empathy, payoff, subtext). Ask what stays unspoken but felt.

### Chapter tracking worksheet
- Keep a thread ledger: main plot, subplot A, subplot B, each marked Advanced, Setup or Resolved for this chapter. A chapter that advances nothing in the ledger is a candidate for cutting or merging.
- Connection tracking, three directions. Previous chapter: immediate continuity, tension carryover, questions resolved, threads advanced. Next chapter: setups planted, tension building toward it, questions raised. Whole novel: major arc advancement, theme development, character progression, world development.
- Character continuity: ask how the character has changed since chapter 1, what new facet this chapter reveals, how relationships shift, and whether behavior is consistent with who they have been.
- POV and voice: check against established patterns, smooth handoffs between viewpoints, depth of penetration into the character's head, balance of internal and external narration.
- Information flow: balance new against established information, stage background revelations, track what the reader knows versus what the character knows, plant future revelations, fold necessary exposition into scene.
- Pacing: compare this chapter's pace with its neighbors, then check internal rhythm, scene length and use of time jumps.

### Failure modes of the analysis itself
- Checklist tyranny: flagging every miss. Before recommending a change ask "does this actually hurt the story?" Some rules can be broken effectively.
- Genre blindness: judging literary fiction by thriller pacing, or action by literary depth. Name the genre contract first.
- Nitpick cascade: catching comma splices while the story has no conflict. Fixing small things first is procrastination from hard work; move to prose only after structure holds.
- Comparison trap: "this isn't as good as [famous work]". A draft is not a finished book; ask what it is trying to do and whether it does it.
- Fix-everything dump: fifty problems make a writer shut down. Give 3-5, with what works named first.

### Worked prioritization
- A draft has a vague premise, three info-dump paragraphs, a POV slip in scene four and two comma splices. Report the missing one-sentence conflict first (structural), mention the info-dumps only as they relate to it, leave the POV slip as optional, skip the commas.
- When structure is sound, the order becomes: shattering moment and emotional payoff, then scene economy, then POV and voice, then sentence level.
