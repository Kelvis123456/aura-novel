# cliche-transcendence
## When to use
Use when a character, scene, or plot element feels generic, "I have seen this before," or exists only to serve the protagonist. It turns a predictable element into a fresh version without losing its function.

## Core rules
- The first ideas that surface are the most available, not the most appropriate. Availability follows frequency of exposure, so first-pass ideas are almost always cliches.
- The goal is conscious choice about which patterns to use and which to transcend, not avoiding every familiar element. Some familiar elements are load-bearing, and some familiarity is reader comfort.
- Apply the Orthogonality Principle: a trope becomes cliche when every aspect matches the default. Change any axis and it feels fresh. The four axes are Form (what is it), Knowledge (what does it know), Goal (what does it want), and Role (what function does it serve).
- The key test: does it know what story it is in? Cliche elements know the plot and act to serve it. Fresh elements have their own concerns and logic that collide with the story rather than serve it, with their own story that intersects.
- Step 1, enumerate cliches: list 5-10 versions seen elsewhere and the genre default. Make the defaults visible, because invisible defaults keep operating.
- Step 2, extract functions: what the element must do for plot, character development, reader information, and emotional experience. Typically 3-5 functions.
- Step 3, generate alternatives for each function: another way to do it, how a different genre would handle it, the opposite that still works.
- Step 4, find unusual combinations: genre collision, tone mismatch (serious plus mundane), scale contrast (cosmic stakes in an intimate place), expectation inversion.
- Step 5, invert perspective: view it through the antagonist's goals, bystanders, institutions and their protocols, or future investigators and the evidence left.
- Step 6, import from other domains: law enforcement, military, medicine, science, business, wildlife biology, sports strategy, history, espionage.
- Step 7, test character specificity: given these characters' skills and psychology, what would they uniquely notice or do? If you can swap in a different character and it works the same, that is a bad sign.
- Step 8, trace downstream consequences: what events it enables, how it changes relationships, what story potential it creates.
- Do not just invert. The opposite of a cliche (evil mentor, hero who fails) sits on the same axis and is often equally tired. Rotate to a different axis instead: a mentor who does not know they are mentoring.
- Changing form alone is not enough ("corporate security instead of FBI" with the same knowledge, goal, and role). At least one of Knowledge, Goal, or Role must change.
- Avoid protagonist orbit, the deepest cliche, where every element exists for the hero. Give elements goals that make sense without the protagonist, so the collision is more interesting than the service.
- Originality is a constraint, not a goal. Every fresh version must still accomplish the original function. Return to Step 2 if novelty drifts from function.

## Diagnose
- What would everyone suggest here, and what is the genre default?
- What must this element actually accomplish?
- Which axes (Form, Knowledge, Goal, Role) match the default?
- Does it know what story it is in?
- Does it exist only to help or obstruct the protagonist?
- Could another character be swapped in with no change?
- Is my alternative just the opposite of the cliche?
- Is it new but useless to the story?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give a diagnosis: list the defaults the passage follows, the functions the element must serve, and which axes are clichéd. Then give a rewritten passage in which at least one axis (Knowledge, Goal, or Role) has been rotated so the element has its own agenda and collides with the story, keeping every function intact. Briefly offer one or two alternative directions after it, without choosing for the writer beyond the rewrite, and note one downstream consequence.
