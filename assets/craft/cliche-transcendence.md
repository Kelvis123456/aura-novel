# cliche-transcendence
## When to use
Use when a character, scene, or plot element feels generic, "I have seen this before," or exists only to serve the protagonist. It turns a predictable element into a fresh version without losing its function.

## Core rules
- The first ideas that surface are the most available, not the most appropriate. Availability follows frequency of exposure, so first-pass ideas are almost always cliches.
- The goal is conscious choice about which patterns to use and which to transcend, not avoiding every familiar element. Some familiar elements are load-bearing, and some familiarity is reader comfort.
- Apply the Orthogonality Principle: a trope becomes cliche when every aspect matches the default. Change any axis and it feels fresh. The four axes are Form (what is it), Knowledge (what does it know), Goal (what does it want), and Role (what function does it serve).
- The key test: does it know what story it is in? Cliche elements know the plot and act to serve it. Fresh elements have their own concerns and logic that collide with the story rather than serve it, with their own story that intersects.
- Step 1, enumerate cliches: list 5-10 versions seen elsewhere and the genre default. Make the defaults visible, because invisible defaults keep operating.
- Step 2, extract functions: what the element must do for plot, character development, reader information, and emotional experience. Typically 3-5 functions.
- Step 3, generate alternatives for each function: another way to do it, how a different genre would handle it, the opposite that still works.
- Step 4, find unusual combinations: genre collision, tone mismatch (serious plus mundane), scale contrast (cosmic stakes in an intimate place), expectation inversion.
- Step 5, invert perspective: view it through the antagonist's goals, bystanders, institutions and their protocols, or future investigators and the evidence left.
- Step 6, import from other domains: law enforcement, military, medicine, science, business, wildlife biology, sports strategy, history, espionage.
- Step 7, test character specificity: given these characters' skills and psychology, what would they uniquely notice or do? If you can swap in a different character and it works the same, that is a bad sign.
- Step 8, trace downstream consequences: what events it enables, how it changes relationships, what story potential it creates.
- Do not just invert. The opposite of a cliche (evil mentor, hero who fails) sits on the same axis and is often equally tired. Rotate to a different axis instead: a mentor who does not know they are mentoring.
- Changing form alone is not enough ("corporate security instead of FBI" with the same knowledge, goal, and role). At least one of Knowledge, Goal, or Role must change.
- Avoid protagonist orbit, the deepest cliche, where every element exists for the hero. Give elements goals that make sense without the protagonist, so the collision is more interesting than the service.
- Originality is a constraint, not a goal. Every fresh version must still accomplish the original function. Return to Step 2 if novelty drifts from function.

## Diagnose
- What would everyone suggest here, and what is the genre default?
- What must this element actually accomplish?
- Which axes (Form, Knowledge, Goal, Role) match the default?
- Does it know what story it is in?
- Does it exist only to help or obstruct the protagonist?
- Could another character be swapped in with no change?
- Is my alternative just the opposite of the cliche?
- Is it new but useless to the story?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give a diagnosis: list the defaults the passage follows, the functions the element must serve, and which axes are clichéd. Then give a rewritten passage in which at least one axis (Knowledge, Goal, or Role) has been rotated so the element has its own agenda and collides with the story, keeping every function intact. Briefly offer one or two alternative directions after it, without choosing for the writer beyond the rewrite, and note one downstream consequence.


## Deep notes
The four axes in detail, with the cliche setting and the orthogonal setting on each.
- Form, what the element is: the expected element. Orthogonal form is the same element; form alone is never the lever.
- Knowledge, what it knows: the cliche knows about the central plot. The orthogonal version has its own concerns and meets the plot by accident.
- Goal, what it wants: the cliche wants to help or stop the protagonist. The orthogonal version wants something unrelated that happens to collide.
- Role, what function it serves: the cliche exists for the protagonist. The orthogonal version has its own story that intersects.
Run the check axis by axis and write down which are default. One axis rotated is usually enough. Zero rotated means the element is a cliche regardless of how it looks.

Worked example. A writer has FBI agents investigating a protagonist who found alien evidence. FBI plus UFO is a highly available pairing. The agents know they are in a UFO story, want to stop the hero, and exist as the obstacle: all three axes default. Rotate Knowledge and Goal: they are working a wire-fraud or missing-persons case, and the protagonist's evidence collides with it. Their opposition now comes from reasonable investigation, not plot service. They are confused why nothing fits, because they believe they are in a different story. The function (pressure on the hero, a ticking clock, an institution the hero must evade) survives intact. Swapping the FBI for "corporate security" with the same knowledge, goal, and role would change only the uniform.

Rotate, do not invert. Evil mentor instead of wise mentor, or a hero who fails instead of succeeds, stays on the same axis and is often equally tired, because "subverted expectations" has become its own default. Move to another axis: a mentor who does not know they are mentoring and is pursuing their own goal that incidentally teaches the hero.

Step-by-step working method, with prompts to ask.
- Step 1, enumerate: write 5 to 10 versions you have seen elsewhere, then the genre default, then the first thing that came to mind. Make defaults visible. Skipping this means you may land on the second most common version and call it fresh.
- Step 2, extract functions: separate form from job. Ask what it must do for plot, for character development, for reader information, for emotional experience. Expect three to five functions. Mark which are load-bearing.
- Step 3, alternatives per function: for each function, brainstorm other ways to do it, how a different genre would do it, and the opposite that still works.
- Step 4, unusual combinations: genre collision (thriller plus literary), tone mismatch (serious plus mundane), scale contrast (cosmic stakes in an intimate place), expectation inversion used as one input, not the answer.
- Step 5, invert perspective: the antagonist's logic, a bystander's attention, an institution's protocols, a future investigator reading the evidence left behind.
- Step 6, import domains: law enforcement, military, medicine, scientific research, business, wildlife biology, sports strategy, historical events, espionage. Ask how that field would handle this exact situation, and what procedure or vocabulary it brings.
- Step 7, character specificity: given these skills and this psychology, what would they uniquely notice or do? If another character could be swapped in with no change, it fails.
- Step 8, downstream consequences: what events it enables or requires, how it changes relationships, what story potential it creates. A fresh choice can create new problems; check them before committing.

Degradation signals that you are misapplying the process.
- Transcending everything. Some familiarity is reader comfort, and some genre elements are required conventions.
- Novelty with no function. If the new version cannot do the job the old one did, go back to Step 2.
- The element was fine and the problem is elsewhere, such as pacing or stakes.
- Repeated transcendence of the same element, which usually means its function is still unclear.
- Transforming an element so it no longer fits the character or the world's rules.

What stays with the writer. Which functions are essential, which alternatives resonate with this story, and when a cliche is an intentional choice. Offer options and name the trade-offs rather than choosing. When a rewrite relies on the enumeration being thorough, say so: if the defaults were not fully listed, the "fresh" version may still be a cliche.

Protagonist orbit check. List every secondary element in the scene and ask what each wants when the protagonist is offstage. If the answer is "to help or block the hero," it orbits. Give it a want and a schedule of its own. The collision with the hero is then a cost both sides pay, not a service one provides. A world where every element resolves into the hero's needs feels thin and artificial.
