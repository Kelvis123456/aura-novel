# key-moments
## When to use
Use when plotting feels mechanical, when the emotional beats of a story need defining, or when building a story from vivid scenes rather than from a plot outline.

## Core rules
- Define the story by the emotional experiences it delivers, not by plot mechanics. Identify the key moments the genre requires, sequence them for impact, then build everything else to make those moments possible.
- Work backward from vivid scenes to what must exist (world, characters, events) to make them happen. Do not outline first and then insert emotion; retrofitted emotion feels mechanical.
- Seven principles: emotional experience primacy; systemic integration with the world; character function alignment; visual-experiential priority (conceive moments as vivid scenes first); flexible sequencing; consequence cascades; bridging efficiency.
- Pick a primary and a secondary elemental genre. Choose 3-5 essential moments from the primary genre and 2-3 supporting ones from the secondary. Secondary supports; it must not overshadow the primary.
- Wonder moments: Initial Encounter, Scale Revelation, Perspective Shift, Wonder Escalation, Transcendent Integration.
- Mystery moments: Question Inception, Pattern Recognition, False Resolution, Progressive Revelation, Solution Crystallization.
- Adventure moments: Threshold Crossing, Capability Test, Resource Depletion, Ultimate Challenge, Return Transformation.
- Horror moments: Wrongness Glimpse, Safety Violation, Threat Escalation, Failed Solution, Confrontation.
- Thriller moments: Stakes Establishment, Deadline Imposition, Near Miss, Option Elimination, Decision Under Duress.
- Relationship moments: Significant Connection, Intimacy Deepening, Value Conflict, Relationship Crisis, Reconciliation/Resolution.
- Drama moments: Internal Conflict Revelation, External Pressure Point, Failure Moment, Truth Confrontation, Character Evolution.
- Issue moments: Perspective Challenge, Stake Personalization, Complexity Recognition, Position Testing, Perspective Integration.
- Ensemble moments: Group Formation, Role Establishment, Group Fracture, Collective Challenge, Synergy Moment.
- Visualize each moment concretely with sensory detail, and define how it changes a character or the reader's understanding of the world.
- Assign character functions (who experiences, enables or opposes each moment) and make the protagonist experience the most significant ones.
- Bridge scenes must serve at least two purposes: move toward the next moment AND develop character, reveal world or explore theme. Compress or cut logistics-only bridges.
- Plant setups early so later moments pay off; foreshadowing raises impact.
- Limit key moments to 5-8 per story. Let some scenes be quiet so peaks feel taller (avoid moment inflation).
- Every moment must follow from established character and world. If a character must act against their nature to reach it, the causation is forced.

## Diagnose
- What emotional experience should the reader have here, and does this moment deliver it?
- Does the moment belong to the primary genre, or has a secondary genre taken over?
- Does it follow logically from who the character is and how the world works?
- Is it vivid and concrete, or only a plot event?
- Does the emotional sequence progress, or repeat the same feeling?
- Are the bridges doing double duty, or just moving characters around?
- Was the setup planted early enough?
- Is everything treated as climactic?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give a short diagnosis: name the genre and the key moment type the passage is attempting, the emotional experience it should create, and where it falls short (mechanical, mismatched, inflated, forced, or logistics-only). Then give a rewritten passage that makes the moment vivid and sensory, builds it from character and situation, lets the emotion emerge instead of being announced, and, if the passage is a bridge, gives it a second function. Keep the author's characters, events and voice. If the user supplies only a premise, return a short list of 5-8 key moments with emotional experience, a one-line scene concept each, and the bridge needs between them.

## Deep notes
This approach follows Robert Rodriguez's habit of imagining the vivid, memorable scenes first, combined with elemental genres. The process has five phases: identify and sequence moments, integrate the world, assign character functions, design connective tissue, then test and refine. Work them in that order: genre before moments, moments before character functions, functions before bridges.

The emotion and job of each moment, by genre (the names are listed above; this is what each must deliver):
- Wonder: Initial Encounter gives surprise and awe and establishes the spectacular concept. Scale Revelation gives a humbling sense of vastness and places the protagonist in it. Perspective Shift is a paradigm change that forces a reevaluation of assumptions. Wonder Escalation intensifies awe and raises stakes. Transcendent Integration makes meaning from the wonder and gives thematic resolution.
- Mystery: Question Inception activates curiosity and sets the puzzle. Pattern Recognition gives the satisfaction of connection and momentum. False Resolution surprises through misdirection and extends the story. Progressive Revelation deepens understanding toward the solution. Solution Crystallization gives illumination and closure.
- Adventure: Threshold Crossing is the excitement of departure into the adventure world. Capability Test gives confidence from competence. Resource Depletion creates vulnerability and forces adaptation. Ultimate Challenge mixes fear and determination at the limit. Return Transformation gives pride and perspective.
- Horror: Wrongness Glimpse is unease from dissonance. Safety Violation is the shock of a breached boundary. Threat Escalation builds dread. Failed Solution brings despair when the obvious defense does not work. Confrontation is terror meeting courage.
- Thriller: Stakes Establishment creates concern for the outcome. Deadline Imposition adds anxiety from time pressure. Near Miss gives relief with lingering tension and keeps peaks and valleys. Option Elimination mounts pressure toward harder choices. Decision Under Duress is catharsis through action.
- Relationship: Significant Connection is recognition of potential. Intimacy Deepening is warmth from vulnerability. Value Conflict is frustration from real differences. Relationship Crisis is heartbreak or betrayal that tests the bond. Reconciliation or Resolution is emotional closure.
- Drama: Internal Conflict Revelation shows a contradiction in the character. External Pressure Point forces choices. Failure Moment brings shame from inadequacy. Truth Confrontation is painful self-awareness that catalyzes change. Character Evolution is self-actualization shown in behavior.
- Issue: Perspective Challenge is intellectual discomfort, Stake Personalization makes the abstract concrete, Complexity Recognition prevents a simplistic resolution, Position Testing examines values, Perspective Integration gives nuance. Ensemble: Group Formation is belonging potential. Role Establishment defines identity within the group. Group Fracture tests loyalty. Collective Challenge forces shared adversity. Synergy Moment shows strength through unity.

Phase details:
- World integration: for each moment ask what must exist for it to occur, what causal chain links world systems to it, and what consequences it leaves behind. A moment that needs a rule of the world should have that rule established before it fires.
- Character functions: each moment needs someone to experience it, someone to enable it and, usually, someone to oppose it. Give arcs that intersect the moments so growth makes the next moment reachable. The protagonist takes the most significant ones.
- Bridges: ask what time, distance and knowledge gaps must be filled, and what character and world state must change between moments.
- Testing: check the emotional journey for gaps and redundancies; check causal logic (does each moment follow from what precedes it, do the character's decisions make sense); check genre satisfaction (are the primary genre's experiences most prominent, does the secondary support rather than overshadow).

Sequencing guidance: start chronological, then consider the tension curve and whether non-linear order would increase impact. Aim for emotional variety: two adjacent moments that deliver the same feeling (two Near Misses, two reconciliations) should be merged or separated by contrast.

Worked example, Wonder primary with Mystery secondary. An oceanographer finds bioluminescent patterns that seem to be a communication system. Wonder moments: Initial Encounter (synchronized patterns across different species), Scale Revelation (the pattern spans the ocean, implying a global network), Wonder Escalation (first artifacts of an ancient civilization), Transcendent Integration (communication with the surviving consciousness). Mystery moments: Question Inception (why did this civilization vanish from human awareness), False Resolution (evidence that it destroyed itself), Solution Crystallization (it evolved beyond physical form). Functions: wonder experiencer (the protagonist), mystery solver (a cryptographer partner), opposition (an agent who wants to weaponize it), skeptic (a scientific body demanding proof), knowledge keeper (a mentor with folklore). Bridges: funding trouble forcing creative methods, the protagonist and partner's relationship, rising outside interest, and progressive decoding that yields partial clues.

Failure modes and fixes:
- Plot first, emotion later: restart from the feelings you want and ask what situation, character and world would produce each.
- Genre mismatch: a horror book whose most prominent beats are relationship beats satisfies neither audience. If the mismatch is deliberate, the story is a different genre than assumed.
- Forced causation: walk backward from the moment and find the events that make it inevitable. If no path exists, change the moment or change the character or world.
- Inflation: if every scene is a peak, demote some to quiet scenes.
