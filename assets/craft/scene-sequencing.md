# scene-sequencing
## When to use
Use when individual scenes work but do not accumulate, when pacing feels rushed or slow, when transitions feel mechanical, or when readers can follow the story but are not compelled forward.

## Core rules
- Treat the scene-sequel pair as the basic unit of pacing. Scenes create tension; sequels process it. Their alternation makes the peaks and valleys that keep a story readable.
- Build every scene as Goal, Conflict, Disaster.
- Goal: the POV character wants something specific and concrete, achievable within the scene, tied to the larger story goal, and clear to the reader within the first beats. Establish it in the first paragraph.
- Conflict: opposition to the goal that escalates. It can be another character with a different agenda, an environmental obstacle or time pressure, or internal resistance such as fear, doubt or values. Each beat must make the goal harder. Static conflict is boring.
- Disaster: end the scene with one of these outcomes, in order of narrative power: "Yes, but..." (goal achieved, new problem created; strongest), "No, and furthermore..." (goal failed, situation worse), "No" (goal failed, must try again), "Yes" (clean success; use sparingly because it kills tension).
- The disaster must be a logical consequence of the scene's conflict, never an authorial intervention. If you cannot trace the chain backward from disaster to conflict, the disaster is arbitrary.
- Build every sequel as Reaction, Dilemma, Decision.
- Reaction: the emotional response to the disaster. It lets the reader process, connect with the character's feelings and breathe. It can be one sentence or pages.
- Dilemma: a genuine choice with no good options. The disaster has closed some paths, revealed information and created competing priorities.
- Decision: the character commits to an action, and that action becomes the goal of the next scene. This is the chain that carries momentum.
- Control tempo with the scene-to-sequel ratio. More scene gives fast, action-heavy, thriller feel; more sequel gives slow, reflective, literary feel. Scenes run at natural length; sequels are the pacing lever, so compress or expand them.
- Match writing mode to function. Action and dialogue serve scene conflict. Description slows pace and suits scene openings and sequels. Introspection belongs in sequels. Summarization compresses time between scenes. Action in a sequel feels rushed; introspection in an action scene kills momentum.
- Do not demand a sequel after every scene and do not prescribe scene lengths; pacing varies. But never eliminate sequels in a fast story: compress them to a paragraph.
- Avoid the Relentless Scene (all conflict, no processing, reader goes numb), the Wallowing Sequel (introspection circling without reaching a decision), the Arbitrary Disaster, the Clean Victory and the Missing Goal.

## Diagnose
- What does the POV character want in this scene, and is it clear early?
- Does the opposition escalate beat by beat, or stay at one level?
- Is the outcome too clean? What "but" or "and furthermore" is missing?
- Does the disaster follow logically from the conflict?
- Did the story process the previous scene, even briefly?
- Is the sequel wallowing, with no movement toward a decision?
- Does the decision lead directly to the next scene's goal?
- Is the scene-to-sequel ratio what the intended pacing needs?
- Does each passage use the writing mode that fits its function?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give a short diagnosis: label the passage scene or sequel, name its goal, conflict and disaster (or reaction, dilemma and decision) as found, say which elements are missing or weak, and state the pacing effect. Keep it to a few lines, most important problem first. Then give a rewritten passage that fixes those problems: state the goal early, escalate the conflict, change the outcome to a "Yes, but..." or "No, and furthermore..." where the ending is clean, or add or compress a sequel as the pacing needs. Keep the author's characters, events, voice and point of view; change only what the diagnosis requires. Do not decide what happens in the story beyond the minimum needed to fix structure. End with one line naming what the next scene's goal now is, if the decision is on the page.

## Deep notes
The framework comes from Dwight Swain's scene-and-sequel model. A scene is outward and dramatized (Goal, Conflict, Disaster); a sequel is inward and processing (Reaction, Dilemma, Decision). The Decision of one sequel is the Goal of the next scene, so the chain is Goal, Conflict, Disaster, Reaction, Dilemma, Decision, new Goal. When a draft feels flat, find the link where the chain breaks.

Goal checks:
- Test the goal with one sentence: "[Character] wants to [concrete action] before/so that [reason]." If the sentence needs an abstract noun (freedom, closure, respect), the goal is not yet concrete enough to oppose.
- A scene goal is smaller than the story goal and serves it. "Get the ledger from the clerk's office" serves "prove the mayor is skimming." A scene goal that merely restates the story goal makes every scene feel the same.
- If the reader learns the goal only halfway through, the early beats read as aimless. Move the want to the first paragraph, even as a single line of thought or a spoken request.

Conflict checks:
- Escalation means each beat raises cost or lowers options. A usable pattern: first obstacle is a refusal, second is a counter-demand, third is a threat or a clock, fourth removes the character's best tool. If three beats in a row are the same obstacle repeated louder, the conflict is static.
- Internal conflict counts only when it changes what the character does on the page (hesitation, a wrong word, a skipped step). Resistance that is only described does not escalate.

Disaster checks:
- Rank by use. "Yes, but..." gives the reader relief and dread at once. "No, and furthermore..." is the escalator for the middle of a book. A plain "No" works when the retry will cost more. A plain "Yes" belongs at a deliberate rest point or the very end.
- Do not run the same disaster type three scenes in a row. Alternate, for example No-and-furthermore, Yes-but, No.
- Backward trace: write the disaster, then list the two or three conflict beats that produced it. If a beat is missing, add it to the scene rather than adding a coincidence.

Sequel checks:
- Reaction comes in a fixed order: body and feeling first, then thought, then the search for options. Skipping to thought makes the character read as cold.
- A dilemma needs at least two options that each cost something. If one option is obviously better, the dilemma is fake; either make the good option carry a hidden price or remove it.
- A decision must be an action the character can attempt next, not a mood. "She resolved to be braver" is not a decision. "She would ask the clerk's rival for the key" is.
- Sequel length scales with the disaster's size: a minor setback earns a line or a paragraph, a betrayal or death can earn pages. In a fast book, compress the three parts into a few sentences and keep all three.

Pacing diagnostics with rough thresholds:
- Count how much of a chapter is scene versus sequel material. Above roughly two-thirds scene reads action-heavy; below roughly one-third reads reflective; the band between is balanced. A long stretch (about 500 words or more) with almost no scene material is a warning sign for wallowing.
- A chapter with heavy dialogue and no reaction, dilemma or decision words often means sequels are missing, not that the pacing is good.

Worked before and after. Before: Mara goes to the harbor office, asks for the manifest, the clerk hands it over, she leaves. Diagnosis: goal implied, no conflict, clean "Yes". After: Mara needs the manifest before the 6 pm tide closes the office; the clerk says only the harbor master can release it; she offers a bribe and he names a price twice her purse; she trades her mother's ring; he hands it over, but the stamp on the page is dated the day before the ship sailed, which means someone forged it first. That ends Yes-but. The sequel can be a few lines: her stomach drops, either she confronts the forger and loses the manifest's value as proof, or she says nothing and sails with a lie in her hands. She chooses to follow the forger's trail to the dockside tavern, which is the next scene's goal.

Edge cases:
- Two scenes can share one sequel when the second goal follows at once.
- A scene can hold a mini-sequel in the middle when the character gets a partial disaster and must choose in the moment.
- A sequel can be dramatized through dialogue with an ally, but the dilemma must still be spoken as a real choice.
- Opening chapters may begin with a sequel-like state (a character already reacting) so long as the goal arrives quickly.
- Summarization bridges time; use it when nothing changes in goal or stakes, never to skip a disaster.
