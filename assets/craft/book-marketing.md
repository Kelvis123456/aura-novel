# book-marketing
## When to use
Use when an author is preparing to publish a novel and needs sales copy: a back-cover blurb, a store description, taglines, a one-line hook, a query or pitch paragraph, or comparable titles. Also use when existing copy reads like a book report, signals the wrong genre, or fails to get clicks.

## Core rules
- Marketing copy promises an experience; it does not summarize a story. Aim to create desire, not to convey information.
- Ask "why will readers regret not reading this?" rather than "what happens in this book?"
- Before writing, establish: elemental genre, emotional core, protagonist, what they want, what stops them, what they risk losing, and 3-5 comparable titles.
- Write a logline first (25-35 words: protagonist, situation, stakes), then expand it into longer copy.
- Make the first sentence a hook that stops a browser. Show stakes within the first 50 words.
- Name the protagonist and ground them with emotional context, not only plot context.
- State stakes as personal emotional loss, not just plot events.
- Stop at the moment of choice. No spoilers past the first act; end on tension or a question, never on resolution.
- Replace abstract adjectives with one concrete, only-in-this-book detail ("dangerous" becomes what makes it dangerous).
- Match the copy's tone and vocabulary to the book's voice and genre. Promise only what the book delivers.
- Genre promises: wonder = awe and discovery; horror = dread; mystery = a puzzle with clues; thriller = stakes and a countdown; romance = emotional connection and satisfying resolution; adventure = what happens next; drama = transformation; humor = wit; idea = perspective shift.
- Back-cover blurb: 150-200 words in three parts: hook (25-50), setup (50-75), stakes question (25-50).
- Store description: 200-400 words, bold opening hook, short paragraphs of 2-3 sentences, one italic stakes line at most, comp titles at the end, key information within the first 100 characters (mobile-first).
- Tagline or hook: under 10 words (15 absolute maximum), no filler words, rhythm tested by reading aloud, memorable after one reading, impossible to apply to another book.
- Query or pitch paragraph: 150-200 words: hook (1-2 sentences), setup (50-75), stakes (50-75), then a meta line with title, word count, genre and comps.
- Comparable titles: 2-4 maximum, published in the last 5 years, same category and tone, recognizable but not mega-bestsellers. Use one for tone and one for concept.
- Avoid the named failure modes: Synopsis Trap, Vague Intrigue ("nothing is as it seems"), Spoiler Reveal, Feature List ("features magic, romance, adventure"), Throat Clear (backstory before story), Wrong Voice, Author Intrusion ("I wrote this because...").
- In pitches avoid rhetorical-question hooks, "In a world where..." openings, vague stakes, and plot-complete synopses.
- Make a distinct version for each platform rather than reusing one text everywhere.
- Never promise sales outcomes. The author owns and approves the copy.

## Diagnose
- Is this a synopsis ("first X, then Y, then Z") or a promise of an experience?
- Does the first sentence create curiosity, and do stakes appear within 50 words?
- What exact genre signals does the copy send, and do they match the book and the comps?
- Which detail appears only in this book? Is it on the page?
- Is the protagonist named, grounded and emotionally framed?
- Does the copy end on tension, or does it reveal an outcome?
- Are there abstract adjectives or empty intrigue that a concrete image could replace?
- Is the length and format right for this platform?
- Could the tagline be quoted from memory, and does it have rhythm aloud?
- Does the voice of the copy echo the voice of the book?

## How to apply
Reply in the language of the request. If the author gives a manuscript summary or existing copy, first name the problem in one or two lines (for example "synopsis trap: lists events, no stakes"), then deliver the rewrite. If they give nothing usable, ask only for the missing core facts (genre, protagonist, want, obstacle, stakes, comps) or infer them from the supplied material and say what you assumed. Produce exactly the deliverable requested, labeled, with its word count; when asked for taglines give 5-8 variants of different types. Offer a logline first when none exists. Keep drafts ready to paste, followed by at most three short notes on choices the author may want to change. Never invent reviews, awards or quotes; leave a clearly marked placeholder instead.

## Deep notes
Workflow. Start by identifying the state of the copy. No copy at all: identify genre, emotional core, stakes in one sentence and at least one comp, then logline, then blurb, then platform versions. Copy that summarizes: find the emotion and the cost of failure, then rewrite the opening as a hook. Wrong genre signal: audit the words (cozy mystery signals quirky, charming, amateur sleuth, small town; thriller signals high stakes, countdown, survival) and check whether the comps match the real book. Generic copy: find the one specific detail. Platform mismatch: split into versions. Forgettable tagline: generate variants and cut.

Weak versus strong. Weak: "Kira discovers she has magic. She goes to a school where she learns to control it. Then she uncovers a conspiracy." Strong: "Kira has spent sixteen years hiding her fire, from the authorities who would execute her, from the family who would disown her, even from herself." The strong version has a name, a concrete image, three layers of stakes and no plot list. Another strong hook pattern: "When Detective Maya Chen finds her own fingerprints at a murder scene she doesn't remember visiting, she has 72 hours to solve the case or become its next victim." A specific, odd detail plus a clock.

Blurb template. Hook paragraph: character, situation, one intriguing detail. Setup paragraph: what they want, what stops them, what complicates it. Stakes paragraph: what they risk, then a question or choice that demands resolution. Genre variations: romance leads with the emotional situation and promises both journey and resolution, naming both leads for dual POV; thriller puts stakes in sentence one with a ticking clock; mystery makes the puzzle the hook and promises fair clues without revealing method; fantasy and science fiction ground in character before world, at most one worldbuilding detail in the hook; literary fiction leads with voice and internal conflict.

Store description formatting. Opening hook in bold, one or two short paragraphs for situation and conflict, an italic stakes line, a "Perfect for fans of" list of 2-4 titles, optional short editorial quotes under 25 words. Use bold only for hook, subheads and the comps header. Check: under 400 words, scannable on a phone, genre signals accurate, no spoilers. If the author can test, vary the opening hook, comp combination and stakes placement.

Tagline types. Promise (emotion verb plus experience: "A love story that will break you and rebuild you"); question ("How far would you go to bury the truth?"); contrast ("She hunts monsters. She is one."); comparison ("The Night Circus meets The Hunger Games", "Pride and Prejudice with dragons"); statement ("Some doors should never be opened."). Best fits: romance and literary lean promise or contrast; thriller and mystery lean question or statement; fantasy leans contrast or comparison; horror leans statement. Process: find the one thing that makes the book different, pick a type, draft 5-10 variants without judging, read aloud, then cut filler words, weak verbs and generic adjectives such as "epic". Sound: parallel structure gives punch, hard consonants (k, t, p) give impact, soft ones (s, l, m) give mystery, alliteration sparingly. For social posts make a family of lengths (about 8, 10, 15 words) that carry the same promise.

Query pitch. Hook states the core conflict and what is unique. Setup names the protagonist, an essential identity (the role most relevant to the story), the situation, the want and the obstacle. Stakes show escalation, the personal loss, and the impossible choice, ending on the choice. Meta line: "TITLE is a 95,000-word YA fantasy, perfect for fans of X and Y." A full query also has a one or two sentence personalization, a short bio of relevant credentials only, and a professional closing. Agents look for voice, clarity, stakes, category fit and polish. Comp formats: "X meets Y", "for fans of X and Y", or one comp plus a twist.

Final checks. Read aloud; confirm the promise matches the book; test the hook on someone who has not read it; verify word counts; confirm no ending is revealed.
