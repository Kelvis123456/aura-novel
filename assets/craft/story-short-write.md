# story-short-write

## When to use
Use for complete short stories of roughly 8,000 to 20,000 words (serialized-platform shorts): emotional pull, tight rhythm, one central twist, compression. All content serves a single target emotion: lingering regret, twist shock, release, warmth, creeping dread, or resonant tenderness.

## Core rules
- Decide the target emotion before the story. Work backward from it, never forward from a vague idea. Every sentence must push plot, set up the twist, or raise emotion; otherwise cut it.
- One twist carries the whole story. No multiple threads, no world-building. All setup feeds the twist and all emotion charges it.
- Default to first person ("I"), the strongest identification. Use third person only when the genre needs multiple viewpoints.
- Narrator stays present and opinionated: it may judge, give the protagonist's subjective verdicts, foreshadow the antagonist's regret, and drop spoiler hooks. Cut only neutral, emotionless author explanation.
- Dual register: during abuse or loss, raw direct venting and rhetorical questions; after the heart "dies" (one standalone declaration line), switch to cold, short, judging sentences.
- Structure by share of length: opening (first 300 to 500 words), setup 30 to 40%, escalation 20 to 30%, twist 10 to 15%, ending 5 to 10%.
- Opening: three sentences decide everything. The first three sentences must be an event, dialogue, action, or information bomb, with no standalone scenery or background. At least three events in the first 100 words. Lead with a quantifiable, specific stake (life, money, child, public humiliation). Hook types: conflict first, information gap, abnormal behavior, uncanny identity, soul-as-observer, suspense sentence, direct question to the reader.
- Setup: build bonds with objects, numbers, habits. Plant at least three clues pointing to the truth, in different sections, kept subtle. Place a hook every two or three sections. Emotion rises section by section; never two sections without an emotional change.
- Escalation: conflict must rise in intensity, scope or cost. Raise hook density to one every two sections. Add misdirection so the reader guesses wrong. Use concrete increasing numbers or amounts. Alternate action and stillness; never two fully violent or fully quiet sections in a row.
- Twist: reveal it within one section, by evidence, witness, overhearing, or peeling layers. Its emotional impact must exceed every earlier peak. Warm up one section before, hold the peak at least two sections, do not drop off after. The reader must be able to trace earlier clues backward.
- Twist position: the reveal should land after about 60% of the story; before 50% it is too early. Set up at least three clues, at least three sections before the reveal. Ensure at least one false trail truly misleads. Every ending element needs earlier setup.
- Recurring object: appears three times (setup, meaning inverted at the twist, callback blow at the end).
- Ending: after the twist, close within about 500 words. End on a quiet detail (an object, an action, one short line), not abstract uplift. Endings: lingering (regret), echo (warmth), open (dread), twist on twist (shock), one-line punch (resonance). Cut empty philosophy ("life is like that").
- Emotion on the page: write the emotion word directly if you like, but follow it immediately with one concrete action or object specific to this moment (the cold soup, the broken jade, the gripped sleeve). Delete stray emotion summaries ("sadness welled up") and repeating the same feeling twice.
- Weave happening, perception, and reaction into one continuous paragraph. Do not write the same moment three times in three paragraphs. Break paragraphs at new action, clue, line of dialogue, or shift of gaze.
- Density: payoffs, humiliations, twists in slow detail; transitions in one or two sentences; setup and daily life in the middle. Never equal weight everywhere.
- Section length: each section at least 800 words (about 50 to 65 short lines); high-density genres such as face-slap or system stories may go down to 500. Section count must match the plan; never merge sections to save length. Never pad: every addition must move emotion, setup, or identification.
- Dialogue takes roughly 40 to 60% in conflict stories (30 to 40% in lyrical tragedies). Three kinds: the aggressor's moral pressure, the rival's soft-knife innocence, and the protagonist's cold verdict after the heart dies. Power shifts show in line length: the aggressor lectures early, the protagonist answers in one line later.
- Every section carries one escalation (a betrayal or reversal), one sharper line, and one new piece of information for the reader. Escalate in steps: moral, then financial, then life-level stakes. Stage humiliation before witnesses.
- Chapter or section ends always leave a hook or aftertaste; do not resolve the emotion.
- Villains escalate from small to large wrongs, with clear motive, weakness and retribution.
- Avoid negate-then-affirm phrasing, generic similes, repeated body-part cliches (cap each at five uses, "like" at ten), ellipses and dashes.

## Planning checklist
- What emotion should the reader feel when finished?
- What is the one-sentence premise: protagonist, predicament, twist, emotional landing?
- What kind of twist (identity, perspective, motive, timeline) and exactly what is revealed?
- At which section does the reader learn the truth, and is that after 60%?
- What are at least three hidden clues, and in which sections?
- What false trail misleads the reader?
- What emotion and intensity (1 to 10) at opening, middle, twist, ending?
- What recurring object appears three times, and with which meanings?
- Who is the antagonist, with motive, method, weakness, retribution?
- Section plan: function, sub-events (3 to 5), emotion, action or stillness, dialogue density, hook, target words?
- Does every ending element have earlier setup?

## How to apply
Write the story as finished prose, never a summary or outline, section by section in order, two or three sections per call. Use numbered section markers (### 1., ### 2.), one line break between paragraphs, and dialogue on its own lines. Each call must continue from the last 300 to 500 words of the previous text and a short note of what is already revealed, the emotional position, open clues and the next link. Do not recap, restate revealed information, or reopen the opening. Hit every section's minimum length and the whole story's minimum. Build toward the single twist, end within about 500 words after it, and finish with a quiet image or a verdict line. Match the chosen genre voice and language. Output only story text, with no commentary, headings other than section markers, or checklists.
