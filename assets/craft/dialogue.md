# dialogue
## When to use
Use when conversations feel wooden or flat, characters sound alike, characters say exactly what they mean, or an exchange only delivers information. It aims dialogue at doing several things at once.

## Core rules
- Dialogue must do more than one thing at a time or it is too inert for fiction. It should advance plot, reveal character, build tension, shift the relationship, and carry subtext together.
- Work on three layers. Text is what is literally said (character-specific, efficient, natural rhythm). Subtext is what is meant beneath it. Context is what shapes the exchange: power, history, what each character wants.
- Apply the Double-Duty Test: for each exchange, answer at least three of four. What does it do for plot? What does it reveal about character? What is the subtext? How does it affect the relationship?
- Make something change by the end of a conversation. The scene should end at a different emotional point than it began.
- Give each character a hidden agenda in every conversation: something they want and cannot ask for directly. The gap between said and meant is where drama lives.
- Show emotion through behavior, not declaration. Convert "I'm angry" or jealousy into indirect expression (a comment on someone's "nice corner office"). Let body language contradict words.
- Ask what each character would never admit out loud.
- Differentiate voices: vocabulary range, sentence complexity, directness (blunt versus circumlocutory), a verbal tic, and a verbal avoidance or topic they dodge. Emotional state should change each character's speech differently. Cover the tags: speakers should still be identifiable.
- Avoid wooden speech. Use contractions, fragments, interruptions, stumbles where emotion calls for them, and characters occasionally talking past each other. Dialogue is compressed reality, not transcribed speech: cut the "Um, hi" small talk unless it reveals character.
- Avoid "As you know, Bob" exposition. Put information inside conflict: let characters disagree, get facts wrong and be corrected, discover things on the page, and spread facts across scenes.
- Do not let characters state themes or lessons (the Philosopher). Trust readers to infer.
- Avoid the Tennis Match of evenly sized alternating replies. Let one character dominate, one interrupt, one stay silent.
- Use "said" as the default tag. Do not let tags do dialogue's job ("she said suspiciously"); use an action beat instead. The beat shows, the tag tells.
- Match pace to tension. Fast: short exchanges, few or no tags, no beats, interruptions. Slow: longer speeches, described pauses, beats between lines, reflection. Use silence deliberately.
- Arguments: characters talk past each other, escalate by repetition, old grievances surface, unsayable things get said. Confrontations: explicit power dynamics, a threat beneath civility. Seduction: saying one thing and meaning another, testing and responding. Lying: the liar believes their own truth and stays consistent with character.

## Diagnose
- Cover the tags: can I tell who speaks?
- Do the lines sound like something a person would say aloud?
- Is anyone explaining something both already know?
- Does anyone say exactly what they feel and want?
- How many of the four double-duty questions can I answer?
- What does each character want that they cannot say?
- Does the relationship or power balance shift during the exchange?
- Does the speed of the dialogue match the tension?
- Are tags carrying emotion that the words should carry?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. Start with a brief diagnosis: which layer fails (text, subtext, or context), which problem applies (identical voices, wooden, exposition dump, on-the-nose, single-function, pacing mismatch), and the double-duty answers you could and could not give, quoting the offending lines. Then provide a rewritten passage that gives each speaker a distinct voice, adds a hidden agenda and subtext, replaces emotion tags with beats, and ends the exchange changed. Keep the same events, information, and characters. End with one or two lines listing what you changed.
