# dialogue
## When to use
Use when conversations feel wooden or flat, characters sound alike, characters say exactly what they mean, or an exchange only delivers information. It aims dialogue at doing several things at once.

## Core rules
- Dialogue must do more than one thing at a time or it is too inert for fiction. It should advance plot, reveal character, build tension, shift the relationship, and carry subtext together.
- Work on three layers. Text is what is literally said (character-specific, efficient, natural rhythm). Subtext is what is meant beneath it. Context is what shapes the exchange: power, history, what each character wants.
- Apply the Double-Duty Test: for each exchange, answer at least three of four. What does it do for plot? What does it reveal about character? What is the subtext? How does it affect the relationship?
- Make something change by the end of a conversation. The scene should end at a different emotional point than it began.
- Give each character a hidden agenda in every conversation: something they want and cannot ask for directly. The gap between said and meant is where drama lives.
- Show emotion through behavior, not declaration. Convert "I'm angry" or jealousy into indirect expression (a comment on someone's "nice corner office"). Let body language contradict words.
- Ask what each character would never admit out loud.
- Differentiate voices: vocabulary range, sentence complexity, directness (blunt versus circumlocutory), a verbal tic, and a verbal avoidance or topic they dodge. Emotional state should change each character's speech differently. Cover the tags: speakers should still be identifiable.
- Avoid wooden speech. Use contractions, fragments, interruptions, stumbles where emotion calls for them, and characters occasionally talking past each other. Dialogue is compressed reality, not transcribed speech: cut the "Um, hi" small talk unless it reveals character.
- Avoid "As you know, Bob" exposition. Put information inside conflict: let characters disagree, get facts wrong and be corrected, discover things on the page, and spread facts across scenes.
- Do not let characters state themes or lessons (the Philosopher). Trust readers to infer.
- Avoid the Tennis Match of evenly sized alternating replies. Let one character dominate, one interrupt, one stay silent.
- Use "said" as the default tag. Do not let tags do dialogue's job ("she said suspiciously"); use an action beat instead. The beat shows, the tag tells.
- Match pace to tension. Fast: short exchanges, few or no tags, no beats, interruptions. Slow: longer speeches, described pauses, beats between lines, reflection. Use silence deliberately.
- Arguments: characters talk past each other, escalate by repetition, old grievances surface, unsayable things get said. Confrontations: explicit power dynamics, a threat beneath civility. Seduction: saying one thing and meaning another, testing and responding. Lying: the liar believes their own truth and stays consistent with character.

## Diagnose
- Cover the tags: can I tell who speaks?
- Do the lines sound like something a person would say aloud?
- Is anyone explaining something both already know?
- Does anyone say exactly what they feel and want?
- How many of the four double-duty questions can I answer?
- What does each character want that they cannot say?
- Does the relationship or power balance shift during the exchange?
- Does the speed of the dialogue match the tension?
- Are tags carrying emotion that the words should carry?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. Start with a brief diagnosis: which layer fails (text, subtext, or context), which problem applies (identical voices, wooden, exposition dump, on-the-nose, single-function, pacing mismatch), and the double-duty answers you could and could not give, quoting the offending lines. Then provide a rewritten passage that gives each speaker a distinct voice, adds a hidden agenda and subtext, replaces emotion tags with beats, and ends the exchange changed. Keep the same events, information, and characters. End with one or two lines listing what you changed.


## Deep notes
Name the failing layer first. Text failures are undifferentiated voices and wooden delivery. Subtext failures are on-the-nose lines with no hidden agenda. Context failures are unclear power, missing history, or no stake in the exchange. Most dialogue problems are subtext problems: saying what you mean is easy to write and dramatically inert.

The six states and their fixes.
- Identical voices: profile each character's speech separately, then give each a verbal tic and a verbal avoidance. Read each speaker's lines in a row and see whether they sound like one person.
- Wooden: full grammatical sentences, no contractions, no interruption. Cut sentences into fragments where a person would trail off, and let characters step on each other.
- Exposition dump: one character asks questions only so another can explain. Find the conflict inside the information.
- On-the-nose: feelings and wants are stated outright.
- Single-function: the scene moves plot and nothing else.
- Pacing mismatch: leisurely talk in a crisis, or rapid-fire talk in a quiet moment.

Quantified voice checks for a scene with two or more speakers.
- Compare vocabulary overlap between speakers. If more than about 60 percent of their word choices are shared, the voices are converging.
- Compare average sentence length per speaker. If they are within a word or two of each other, differentiate.
- Compare contraction rate. If nobody contracts at all (under about 5 percent of opportunities), the whole scene reads stiff.
- Compare the share of questions versus statements. If every speaker is above half questions, the scene is an interrogation or a quiz. Make someone assert.
- Compare fragment rate. A character who never uses fragments and one who speaks only in them are already distinct; a pair with identical rates are not.
- Filler talk ("um," "well," "yeah") above about 5 percent of lines is too much for page dialogue; above 10 percent it is dead air.

Verbal DNA checklist for each speaker. Vocabulary range (trade jargon, slang, formal Latinate words). Sentence complexity (clauses stacked versus clipped). Directness (blunt versus circling). One recurring tic (a pet word, a habit of answering a question with a question, a trailing "anyway"). One avoidance (the topic they dodge, the word they will not say, the name they never use). An emotional-state shift: nervous, one talks more and the other goes monosyllabic; angry, one gets formal and the other gets profane.

Double-Duty Test in practice. Write the four answers beside each exchange: what it does for plot, what it reveals about character, what the subtext is, how the relationship moves. If you can fill fewer than three, add a second purpose before polishing the lines. Typical upgrade: an information handoff becomes a negotiation where one party withholds, or a favor request where the asker cannot admit need.

Subtext conversions.
- Jealousy: not "I'm jealous of your promotion" but a remark about their "nice corner office."
- Anger: not "I'm furious" but over-polite precision, or doing the dishes too loudly.
- Love or need: a practical question ("Did you eat?") that carries the weight.
- A body that contradicts the words: "I'm fine," while she grips the counter edge.
Build the gap deliberately. Write what each character wants and cannot ask for, then write lines that circle it and never touch it. Ask what each would never admit aloud and make the scene press on exactly that.

Fixing exposition. Before: "As you know, Bob, our company was founded in 1985 when your father and my uncle started it." After: have two characters dispute one detail ("Your father never signed anything." "He signed the lease, that's different."), so the reader pieces the history together from the disagreement. Other moves: let someone learn the fact on the page, let a character get it wrong and be corrected, and spread the backstory across several scenes in single-sentence doses.

Special situations, in more detail.
- Argument: speakers answer the previous insult rather than the previous point. Escalate by repeating phrases. An old grievance surfaces without warning. Someone says the thing that cannot be unsaid, and the scene should register that.
- Confrontation: state or imply the stakes. Power is explicit, and civility carries a threat under it. A winner and loser emerge.
- Seduction of any kind: one thing said, another meant. Testing, then responding. Revelation is gradual, and the unsaid carries the charge.
- Lying: the liar believes their own version. Tells must fit the character, and other characters may or may not catch them. The reader may know more than the listener.

Common failure modes. Characters announcing the theme in a line the reader could infer. A scene that ends where it began emotionally. One character used as an audience stand-in whose only job is to ask. Fixing voice by adding accents or catchphrases instead of vocabulary and rhythm.
