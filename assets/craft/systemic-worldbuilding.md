# systemic-worldbuilding
## When to use
Use when the author introduces a new technology, species, magic, or alternate history and needs to trace how it would realistically transform society across many domains and timescales. It builds the world from one divergence outward.

## Core rules
- Hold to Pohl's principle: a good story predicts not the automobile but the traffic jam. Authenticity lives in consequences, not in the novelty of the change.
- Level 1, Initial Divergence Point: define precisely what differs from our world. Types: speculative technology, historical divergence, alternate physics or functional magic, social innovation, species or biology.
- Level 2, first-order consequences: who gains immediate power or advantage, what existing systems become obsolete, what the initial market and social responses are, what technical limitations and requirements emerge, what the first unintended effects are.
- Level 3, second-order systemic adaptations: economic structures (new markets, dead industries, value shifts), power structures (reinforced or disrupted hierarchies), social behaviors, resistance movements, infrastructure change, exploitation patterns (who benefits, who suffers, who profits from the transition).
- Level 4, third-order cultural evolution: new language and metaphors, ethical frameworks and taboos, belief-system adaptations, normalization of the once extraordinary, artistic responses, educational shifts.
- Level 5, intersection analysis: how the change hits different groups differently across socioeconomic class, geography (urban/rural, rich/poor regions), generation, marginalized communities, and nations.
- Choose depth versus breadth deliberately: deep chains through all levels for chosen domains, or shallow coverage of many domains for an interconnected feel. Use tracer stories (follow one impact through many domains via a character or institution).
- Use three horizons: short-term (years: reactions), medium-term (decades: institutional adaptation), long-term (generations: cultural and psychological shift).
- Focus with character-centered ripples, story-relevant consequences, and thematic filters.
- Mine contradictions: every change has desirable and dangerous aspects, different groups weigh the tradeoffs differently, and rationalizations emerge to justify the downsides. Use friction points as conflict sources.
- Define visibility markers so characters can perceive the change: physical, behavioral, social, and linguistic.
- Classify adaptations as institutional, criminal/underground, pathological, ideological, and technological work-arounds.
- Procedure: define the change, map first-order impacts across all major domains, identify inherent contradictions, explore how groups respond, follow the most interesting chains deeper, look for unexpected intersections between domains, then locate story potential at points of maximum tension.
- Target counts: 2-3 inherent contradictions, 2-3 chains traced to third-order depth, at least 3 intersections (class, geography, generation), and specialized vocabulary for new concepts.
- Avoid the Cool Tech Trap (stopping at first order; keep asking "and then what?"), Monoconsequence Thinking (all good or all bad; for every winner name the loser), Homogeneous Response, Domain Isolation, and No Visibility Markers.

## Diagnose
- Is the divergence point one clear, specific change, or a vague mood?
- Have I stopped at the first order of effects?
- For each benefit I listed, who loses, and for each harm, who profits?
- Does every class, region and generation react identically?
- Have I explored one domain repeatedly while ignoring economy, politics, belief or language?
- What would a character see, hear or smell that proves the change exists?
- What new words, jokes or insults would exist now?
- Which two consequences conflict, and how does society live with that conflict?
- Which chain produces the most tension for this story?

## How to apply
Reply in the language of the request. Return a paste-ready sheet with these headings: Divergence Point (one or two sentences, precise); Consequence Chains (for the two or three richest chains, list order 1, 2 and 3 as arrows with concrete specifics); Domain Impacts (economy, power, social, culture and belief, environment, one short entry each); Intersections (class, geography, generation, margins, nations); Contradiction Map (two or three tensions and how groups rationalize them); Adaptations (institutional, underground, pathological, ideological, technological); Visibility Markers (physical, behavioral, social, linguistic, plus five new terms); Story Potential (the points of maximum tension, ranked). Mark invented elements as proposals, stay consistent with any lore the user supplied, and state which timescale each chain covers.
