# Long-Form Style

## When to use
Use when drafting or polishing serial fiction prose: scene writing, action and face-slapping payoffs, dialogue, removing machine-sounding phrasing, scanning for overused words, and setting paragraph and punctuation format.

## Core rules
- Decide the target emotion of a scene first, then write only what delivers it. Answer the reader's "and then?" at the end of every information point.
- Write as a camera following the protagonist: only what can be seen, heard, smelled, touched. Stay in close limited point of view. No omniscient commentary, no "she did not know that", no summarizing the character's psychology.
- Show emotion through the outside effect, never the emotion word. Ladder from weakest to strongest: stated emotion, inner thought, facial expression, action. Prefer action and body detail.
- Blend what happens, what the character notices and how the body reacts into one continuous passage. Never split one moment into three stacked paragraphs.
- Allocate detail by weight: payoff, face-slap, reversal and emotional peak get dense, slow-motion treatment; transitions, travel and exposition get one or two sentences.
- Break paragraphs by dramatic unit: a new action, new object, new information, new speaker, gaze shift or end of scene. Keep a full chain of reasoning or mood in one longer paragraph. Never vary length mechanically, and never run many identical lengths in a row.
- Fast scenes use short sentences; slow scenes allow long ones. After three short sentences, use a long one, and vice versa.
- Use at least two senses per scene, tied to the emotion. Environment description must interact with a character or it is cut.
- Prefer plain spoken words over formal or classical ones. Short phrases beat idioms.
- Alternate action, dialogue and reaction. Do not run more than two paragraphs of pure inner monologue.
- Every line of dialogue must advance the plot, build tension or show character; in emotional scenes it must also respond to the previous speaker's emotion (accept, deflect, escalate, retreat).
- Make dialogue length track power: the one in control speaks in ten words or fewer, the pressed side speaks twenty or more.
- Give every speaker a distinct voice: catchphrase, rhythm, vocabulary, fixed stance, status, temperament, stage of the relationship.
- Never put true motive in the line; characters speak with excuses and agendas, and the collision of agendas is the tension.
- In action and payoff scenes, write for the payoff: set up the stakes, then release in proportion to the setup, and make the hero never look humiliated. Delay is acceptable, a letdown is not.
- Predetermine the fight result (crush, win as underdog, escape to the next stage) and plan a reversal. Do not write blow-by-blow; write strategy, price and reaction of onlookers.
- Spread shock outward in layers: nearest witnesses first, then the next circle, then the highest authority, then back to the crowd. Each person reacts by their own interest.
- End chapters on an action, line of dialogue or open question, never a summary, moral or forecast.
- Scan finished text against the lists to avoid below. Delete first; only rewrite when deleting loses plot, hook, character or needed information.
- Follow the formatting defaults below unless the user or platform states otherwise.

## Diagnose
- Does each paragraph answer "and then?"
- Where do I tell an emotion that I could show by an action?
- Is the narrator explaining, forecasting or judging instead of living in the character?
- Are all beats equally detailed, flattening the payoffs?
- Could a reader tell speakers apart with names covered?
- Does dialogue only trade information, with no emotional carry-over between lines?
- Did the payoff land at the strength the setup promised?
- Is any sentence a banned pattern or an overused word from the lists?
- Do chapter endings summarize or moralize?

## How to apply
When asked to write a scene, answer in the language of the request. State the target emotion in one line, pick the pacing (dense or sparse beats), then write the scene in close limited view with the formatting defaults. For action, name the result type first. For dialogue, assign each speaker a stance and agenda. When asked to polish, run three passes: strip generic lines, replace formal wording with spoken wording, restore sensory and voice detail. Light cases need only pass one; heavy cases need all three plus rewrites of key passages. Return the revised text, then a short list of patterns removed.

## Deep notes
Weak versus strong. Weak: "He felt a surge of anger and clenched his fists." Strong: "He slapped the chopsticks on the table and soup jumped out of the bowl." Weak: "She was very rich." Strong: "She dropped a card on the table that cost more than the meal." Weak chapter end: "He finally understood that letting go was best." Strong: "He put the cigarette out and went to bed." Weak: "Everyone gasped." Strong: the man across the table spilled his tea; the others looked at each other, one stepped back, someone reached for a phone; the smug woman's smile froze and she opened her mouth and said nothing.

Stacked description, the mistake to avoid: three paragraphs where the father pins the paper, then his hand shakes, then the pen stops and the stroke skews. Merge: "The father pinned the paper with his left hand and set the pen down with his right; the tip skidded as soon as it touched, the shake ran from elbow to wrist, and the first stroke dragged sideways."

Face-slapping framework. Five steps: the hero is strong but unseen, the difficulty is dramatized, the supporting cast doubts him, he solves it easily, everyone is shocked. Three escalations: plain showing off; the Hua Xiong method (a side character raises the villain, the villain raises the hero); hard pressure (press until the reader is hooked, then release). Anger-based pressure (the villain does something infuriating) and contempt-based pressure (someone looks down on the hero) must not be mixed. Cycle a slap three or four times, each stronger. Enlarge the stage: more observers, higher-ranked opponent, more at stake. Add a second-order effect: the display changes someone's interests or plans, so each reaction is personal. The invincible hero never dawdles at the kill.

Fight design. Heart, body, skill: will and nerve, physical limits, techniques. Underdog wins come from will, information gap, terrain, a paid price or psychological play. Repeat moves in one sentence and let onlookers carry the effect; describe a signature move in full only once. Rhythm: fast, slow, fast; build, burst, aftermath. Escalate from fists to spells to mechanics, from numbers to mechanisms. Use time, numbers, intangible elements and allusion to give cultivation duels weight.

Dialogue modes. Suppression: opponent speaks three to five lines, hero answers one word. Reversal: opponent boasts, hero states one fact, silence. Dead heart: replies shrink from explanation to silence to "whatever". Emotional escalation ladder: state the fact; fact plus advice; blame; blame plus command; blame plus belittling. Crowd chatter should progress: ordinary people, experts, special-identity witnesses, emotional lift; vary tones, one idea per line. Avoid characters who lecture settings: split exposition into half-sentences squeezed under pressure. In grave, deadly or grieving beats, jokers and light characters go quiet.

Lists to avoid. Most toxic structures: "not A, but B" (and its variants without the connector); "..., with a touch of ..." as a catch-all modifier; "his voice was not loud, yet carried a force of ..."; "He/She knew ..."; "as if ... as though"; "a flash of ... crossed his eyes", "the corner of his mouth curved into ..."; "a surge of ... rose in his heart", "his heart jolted"; chapter-end forecasts like "what he did not know was ...". Tier-one words and phrases (replace on sight): seemingly, as if, like, a trace of, a touch of, some, a few parts, faintly; took a deep breath, slowly, could not help but, slightly, lightly, faintly (as manner adverbs); flash in the eyes, corner of the mouth lifted, brows slightly knit, eyes lowered, pupils contracted; his heart stirred, heart shook, understood in his heart, said to himself, welled up inside, involuntarily; unquestionable, undeniably, obviously, undoubtedly, hard to notice; resolute, glinting, cunning, deep, biting, icy; unconsciously, helplessly, naturally. Tier-two (replace when frequent): suddenly, as if, in an instant (keep when truly sudden or colloquial); formal words such as "collapse"; summary sentences ("he finally understood", "she then realized", "at this moment", "everything ... all", "it turned out"); parallelisms (three or more same-structure sentences, "some ... some ... some", "one ... one ... one"); elevation sentences ("this moment", "this is"). Also cut: forecasts of the future, "looks promising", "bright future", "full of hope", essay openers ("it is not hard to see", "in fact", "in conclusion"), formal connectors ("thereupon", "meanwhile", "thus", "indeed"), generic metaphors such as animal, object, state, abstract or hypothetical comparisons ("like a wild dog", "like fate's gears", "so hard it seemed to crush bone"), delete by default. Cap weak adverbs (slightly, faintly, slowly, gently) at three per thousand words. Cut mushy meaning inflation ("far-reaching", "unprecedented"). Cut explaining voice: "the reason was", "this meant", "little did she know", "years later", "as if foreshadowing", "she acted so well", "just right" and "just so" evaluations, and punchline similes that conclude for the character. A new term still needs one anchor: show its visible effect on the character, not its backstory.

Formatting defaults. One newline between paragraphs, no blank lines, no indentation, no bold or italics or dividers inside the prose. Dialogue on its own line in the quote style the project uses. Replace dashes and ellipses used as pauses with commas, periods, short sentences, line breaks or an action beat. Match punctuation to tone: pressure and restraint use short sentences and periods; questions use question marks sparingly; a real burst gets one exclamation mark, never several. Do not flatten every sentence to a period, and never scatter marks without function. Vary subject naming: name the protagonist at a paragraph start or turn, use pronouns inside an action chain. Avoid formulaic speech tags; plain "said" may stay. Chapter markers must not appear in the prose.
