# naming
## When to use
Use when a name (character, place, fictional brand, or title) feels off, forgettable, mismatched with its neighbors or hard to read. It diagnoses why across sound, meaning, culture and function.

## Core rules
- Names work on four layers that must align: Sound, Meaning, Cultural and Functional. When they align the name feels inevitable; hidden conflicts between layers make it feel wrong even when no one can say why.
- Sound-meaning links: depth sounds (a, o, u, m, n) suggest weight and gravitas; light sounds (i, e, l, s) suggest speed, precision and elegance; power sounds (k, t, p, x) suggest strength and impact; flow sounds (l, r, w) suggest movement and grace; tech sounds (x, z, -ix, -ex) suggest the modern and digital.
- Phoneme frequency: common sounds (t, n, s, k, m, p, l, r and the basic vowels) feel natural and trustworthy; rare sounds (x, z, q, zh, ü, ø, æ) feel distinctive or exotic. Use common sounds for accessibility and rare ones for distinctiveness; too many rare sounds are unpronounceable.
- Syllable structure: CV is open and flowing (Sora), CVC is solid (Mark), CVCV is balanced and memorable (Roku), CCV is dynamic (Slack).
- Meaning types: descriptive, metaphorical, abstract (coined), portmanteau. The best names work on several levels at once: literal, metaphorical and cultural.
- Cultural layer: follow category conventions to signal belonging, break them on purpose to differentiate. Fantasy convention, for example, is apostrophes and unusual clusters.
- Functional tests: spelling is intuitive from pronunciation and vice versa, typing is comfortable, search returns relevant results, and the name can be said aloud and understood (the phone test).
- Names that belong together share a phoneme inventory (which sounds are "in"), consistent syllable templates and a documented convention. Identify outliers and regenerate them.
- Characters: keep cultural consistency within one world, align sound with character or subvert it knowingly, keep names pronounceable, and make each distinct within the cast.
- Places: keep one linguistic origin per region, so places in the same region have related sounds, and keep them easy to tell apart.
- Titles: signal genre, carry emotional resonance, be memorable and match sound to mood.
- Quick fixes: harsh, add flow sounds or soften consonants; weak, add power sounds or shorten; generic, add a distinctive phoneme or unusual combination; foreign, shift toward high-frequency phonemes; dated, update to current category patterns.
- Avoid: the Kitchen Sink (one name carrying every message; pick one), the Inside Joke (meaning only the author gets), the Sound-Alike (too close to an existing name), the Unpronounceable, and the Apostrophe Catastrophe (random apostrophes; if used, define what they mean).
- Do not approve a name without checking all four layers, and do not impose taste over the story's audience and needs.

## Diagnose
- Do the sounds match the intended emotional tone?
- Does the meaning conflict with the sound or with the context?
- Does it feel like it belongs to its neighbors (same culture, same phoneme palette)?
- Would a reader remember it, and does it have a distinctive hook?
- What does it sound like it should be, and is that what it is?
- Could it carry an unintended or negative association in another language?
- Can a reader pronounce it from the spelling and spell it from hearing?
- Is it too similar to another name in the work?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give a diagnosis: the problem state (does not feel right, names do not belong together, forgettable, wrong signals, impractical) and a layer-by-layer note on sound, meaning, culture and function, stating the conflict you find. Then return a rewritten passage using revised names, with a short mapping of old to new and the reason for each, based on the layer that failed. Offer at most three candidates per name, each with a one-line justification, and say which you recommend. Keep the author's plot, voice and any names that already work.
