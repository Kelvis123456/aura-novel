# character-naming
## When to use
Use when character names cluster around statistical defaults, when the cast has similar or colliding names, or when a fictional culture needs consistent, plausible naming. It breaks the model's habit of picking the most common name.

## Core rules
- Language models default to statistical medians: the most frequent name for each perceived category (the "Chen proliferation": Chen, Patel, Garcia, Kim; first names like Maya, Marcus, Sofia, Aiden). When corrected they "median-hop" to the top name of another ethnicity. Never reach for the first name that comes to mind.
- Draw names from deep in the range of a culture, not from the top. Deliberately use less common but real names, vary initials, syllable counts and rhythms, and do not repeat a surname across unrelated characters.
- Establish context before naming: setting, place, time period, cultures present, naming conventions, which names are already locked in, and who named the character (parents or themselves).
- Contemporary and historical settings: use complete cultural packages. Match the character's generation (immigrant, second generation), background, class and birth period. Do not pair a Japanese surname with a Chinese given name, or give a first-generation immigrant an Anglicized first name their parents would not have chosen.
- Period fiction: use names that existed and were common then and there ("Jennifer" in medieval England and "Jayden" in Victorian London are mismatches). Respect conventions such as patronymics.
- Mixed settings: define the realistic cultural distribution first, based on setting logic, then name per culture. A story in Seoul should not contain one of every culture; a story in London can justify real diversity. Avoid the diversity checkbox of exactly one character per ethnicity.
- Fantasy and science fiction: define the culture's sound rules before naming. Use a consistent phoneme palette and syllable templates. Examples of palettes: flowing, vowel-heavy with diphthongs (elvish-like); guttural, consonant-heavy with hard stops (harsh); balanced and pronounceable (neutral). Fantasy is not random syllables, and "Kael", "Zephyrine" and "Bob" in one kingdom is incoherent.
- Decide the naming convention: patronymic, descriptive, clan-based, use-name, or class-dependent patterns.
- Keep names pronounceable. Limit consonant clusters and test by reading aloud ("Xzylthrix" breaks immersion).
- Prevent cast collision: avoid Sarah/Sara, Mike/Mark/Michael, Lee/Leigh. Vary initial sound, syllable count and stress pattern, and check each new name against everyone already in the cast.
- Check the name against the character: background, era, role, class signals and unwanted associations.
- Keep a running cast list of names used so later names can be checked.
- Do not default to American naming patterns without context.

## Diagnose
- Is the context (place, era, cultures) known well enough to name anyone?
- Do several names look like the statistical center of their category?
- Do multiple characters share a surname or an initial letter?
- Do any two names look or sound alike on the page?
- Do names within one fictional culture follow a shared sound pattern?
- Does each name fit the character's generation, class and origin?
- Is the cast's cultural mix plausible for the setting rather than a checklist?
- Can a reader pronounce every name without effort?

## How to apply to a draft
Reply in the same language as the passage. First give a diagnosis: which problem applies (no context, default clustering, cultural incoherence, cast collision, character mismatch, forced mix), listing the offending names. If context is missing, ask for it briefly and state your assumption. Then return a revised passage with replacement names, plus a compact list mapping old name to new name with a one-line reason each (culture, period fit, distinct initial and rhythm). Draw replacements from less common, plausible names, and make sure no two share an initial or sound. Keep the author's plot, voice and every name that already works.


## Deep notes

### Drawing without a script
- Simulate entropy: build a candidate pool of 15 or more names for the culture, then pick from the back two thirds by an arbitrary rule (the third name from the end, the name whose initial is least used in the cast). Reject the first name that arrived in your head, and reject its obvious substitute from another culture.
- Generation matters: an older cohort takes classic or religious names, a younger cohort takes current fashions. Immigrant families often give a heritage name plus a separate everyday name.

### Four layers of any name
- Sound: depth sounds (a, o, u, m, n) read heavy and serious; light sounds (i, e, l, s) read quick and precise; power sounds (k, t, p) read hard; flow sounds (l, r, w) read smooth. Match sound to the character's temperament or deliberately contrast it.
- Meaning: literal, metaphorical, cultural, personal. A name working on two levels rewards a second reading.
- Cultural: category conventions signal membership, such as apostrophes and odd clusters for fantasy. Follow them to belong, break them on purpose to stand out.
- Functional: can a reader say it, spell it, and tell it from every other name at a glance?
- Syllable shapes: CV open and flowing (Sora, Kano); CVC solid (Mark, Bond); CVCV balanced and memorable; CVCC weighty (Kraft); CCV energetic. Use common sounds for ease and one rare sound for distinction. Too many rare sounds make a name unreadable.

### Fantasy phoneme palettes
- Flowing: consonants l, r, n, m, th, s, v, f; vowels a, e, i, o plus ae, ai, ei, ie, ea; templates CV, CVC, V, VC. Yields Aelindra, Caelorn, Thalion, Miriel, Elowen.
- Harsh: consonants k, g, kr, gr, th, z, zh, dr, sk, st, tr; vowels a, o, u; templates CVC, CVCC, CCV, CCVC. Yields Gruknar, Thokk, Kragash, Vordak, Zugrak.
- Neutral: consonants k, t, s, n, m, l, r, d, v, f, b, p, g, j, h, w, z; vowels a, e, i, o, u, ai, ei, ou; templates CV, CVC, VC. Yields Kiran, Solen, Mira, Daven, Tessa, Jorin.
- Once a palette is chosen, every name in that culture draws only from it, in two to three syllables. Let different classes use different conventions (clan name for nobles, use-name for commoners) and say so.

### Cast tracking checklist
- For each new name record initial letter, syllable count, stress, and role. No two major characters share an initial; no more than three names in a cast of twenty start with the same letter.
- Mix one-, two- and three-syllable names, and vary endings (-a, -n, -s, -o).
- Check how the name looks in dialogue and in the possessive; check whether a nickname will collide with another name.
